# Chapter 1: The_Mill_Race

The sky was the colour of a wet slate, and the air, even at this early hour, was thick with the promise of rain. Nell Ashby pulled her coat collar closer, the damp wool doing little to ward off the chill that had seeped into her bones overnight. She walked briskly along the river path, her sensible shoes squelching on the soft earth, her breath forming small plumes of vapour in the cold air. The town of Lessing Fen was just beginning to stir, the shutters of the cottages on Wick Street still mostly closed against the dawn. Nell was on her way to Mr. Pell’s office, a task she had performed every weekday for the past five years, and she expected nothing out of the ordinary to interrupt the familiar routine.

But the ordinary had clearly been suspended. A small crowd had gathered near the old stone bridge that spanned the mill race. They were a silent, morbid bunch, farmers and labourers mostly, their faces grim as they stared down into the churning, grey water. Nell felt a prickle of unease. She quickened her pace, pushing through the outer ring of onlookers. There, at the water’s edge, Constable Arthur Dorrit was directing two men with a long pole. At the end of the pole, trailing in the current, was a body.

It was Owen Tarrant. Nell knew him by his build, by the shock of white hair plastered to his brow. The men hauled the sodden form towards the bank, their movements practised and unhurried. When the body was laid out on the muddy grass, Nell saw the face, pale and slack, the eyes closed. A woman Ivy, Nell recalled, Tarrant’s widow, was nearby, being held upright by a neighbour, her face a mask of brittle shock. There was no sound except the lapping of the water and the low murmur of the crowd.

“Slipped in the dark, I expect,” Constable Dorrit said, more to himself than to anyone. He was a stout man, with a walrus moustache that seemed perpetually drooping with disappointment. “The stone is treacherous when it’s wet. A man of his age, a nasty shock to the system.” He nodded sagely, as if this pronouncement settled the matter. He glanced at Nell, his gaze flicking over her plain coat and sensible hat. “Morning, Miss Ashby. On your way to work, I presume? Dreadful business. Dreadful.”

Nell said nothing. She looked from the constable’s self-satisfied face to the still form of Owen Tarrant. The mill race was a narrow channel, fed by the river and controlled by the sluice gate at its far end. The gate was wide open, the water roaring through the gap, a sight it rarely wore. Dorrit’s explanation felt thin, a plaster on a wound that looked suspiciously deep. But the coroner was yet to be called, and it was not her place to contradict the town’s law. She murmured a word of condolence to Ivy Tarrant, who did not seem to hear, and then turned away, her earlier sense of purpose now tinged with a cold, sharp curiosity.

* * *

Mr. Pell’s office on the High Street smelled of old paper, lemon polish, and something indefinably stale. It was a small, cluttered room, every surface covered in ledgers, letters, and legal documents. Mr. Pell himself was a small, frail man with spectacles perched on the very end of his nose, and he was currently peering at a document through them as if it were written in a foreign language.

“Ah, Nell,” he said, without looking up. “Good morning. I trust your walk was uneventful.”

Nell hesitated, placing her hat on the hook. “Actually, Mr. Pell. There’s been… an accident at the mill. Mr. Tarrant has been found drowned in the mill race.”

Mr. Pell slowly lowered his spectacles and blinked at her. “Owen? Drowned? Good heavens.” He removed his spectacles entirely and rubbed the bridge of his nose. “I had no idea. How very dreadful. The poor man.” He sighed, a sound like air escaping a punctured bellows. “Well, that explains the letter I was about to dictate. He was my client, of course. One must attend to these things promptly. Nell, I need you to go to Tarrant’s house this afternoon. The Fen & County Bank will be requiring an inventory of the estate for probate. You’ll need to go through his papers, his ledgers, everything. Make a list of his assets and liabilities.”

It was a straightforward task, the kind of work Nell did every week. But the image of Owen Tarrant’s body lying on the mud flashed in her mind. “Of course, Mr. Pell,” she said, her voice steady. “I’ll start this afternoon.”

Just then, the office door opened and a man bustled in. He was of medium height, with neat, mousy hair and a worried, pleasant face. He was dressed in a dark suit that was immaculate save for a small smear of mud on his trouser leg. This was Ambrose Vane, Tarrant’s bookkeeper and junior partner. His geniality seemed to fill the small room.

“Mr. Pell! Miss Ashby,” he said, his voice warm and concerned. “I just came from the mill. It’s just too awful, simply too awful. One moment he was there, the next… gone.” He wrung his hands, a picture of genuine grief. “Mr. Pell, I understand you’ll be handling the estate. I wanted to offer my assistance. I can provide you with access to the house, of course. I have all the keys.” He reached into his pocket and produced a large, old-fashioned iron key on a brass ring. “And I can help you with the ledgers. The bank will need them, naturally, to reconcile the accounts.”

He placed the key on Mr. Pell’s desk. It was a heavy thing, ornate and old. Vane’s own movements were precise, economical. He was the picture of a man in control, trying to bring order to chaos.

“Thank you, Ambrose,” Mr. Pell said, his voice regaining some of its customary authority. “That is very helpful. Nell here will be taking charge of the inventory. She’ll be at the house this afternoon.”

“Excellent,” Vane said, beaming at Nell. “If there’s anything at all I can do, just ask. I know Mr. Tarrant’s affairs inside and out. It’s the least I can do.” He offered a weak smile. “Now, if you’ll both excuse me, I must go and break the news to Ivy again. Dreadful business.”

He bustled out as quickly as he had arrived, leaving the scent of lavender water and a sense of frantic helpfulness in his wake. Nell looked at the key on the desk. It was not the key to the front door, which she already possessed. It was heavier, more functional. A mill key, perhaps. She picked it up. It felt cold in her hand.

* * *

The afternoon light was failing, a weak grey seeping through the clouds. Nell had spent three hours at Tarrant House, a large, cold place filled with the ghost of a man who had clearly been a force of nature. The ledgers were in the study, heavy-bound volumes with columns of figures that spoke of business, of drainage boards, of profit and loss. Vane had been right; he did know the affairs inside and out. He had guided her to the exact shelves, his manner solicitous, his eyes bright with a kind of nervous energy. He had mentioned the Fen Drainage Board fund several times, explaining how Tarrant’s mill managed it on their behalf.

Her work done, Nell decided to walk back along the river path, the longer route home. It was quieter now, the town behind her. The roar of the mill race grew louder as she approached the stone bridge. She stopped, leaning on the parapet, and watched the water thunder through the open sluice gate. The sight was unnerving. It was an unnecessary, violent release of water. Dorrit’s story of a slip seemed even less likely now. Why would the gate be open?

A voice, low and quiet, spoke from behind her. “It wasn’t an accident, was it?”

Nell turned. A man was standing a few feet away, leaning on a wooden post. He was tall and broad-shouldered, with a face that was weathered and impassive. He wore a rough tweed jacket and a pair of muddy boots. His eyes, a pale grey, were fixed on the churning water.

“I’m sorry?” Nell said.

“The mill owner,” the man said, nodding towards the body’s earlier resting place. “They say he slipped. But the sluice was opened. You don’t open the sluice for a bit of rain.” He introduced himself as Kit Merrow, the keeper of the Lessing lock and the sluice. His voice was flat, devoid of emotion, but there was an intensity in his gaze.

“Why do you say the sluice was opened?” Nell asked, intrigued.

“Because it wasn’t opened by the flood board,” Kit said, pushing himself off the post. He walked towards the sluice gate, a massive structure of oak and iron. He pointed to a keyhole set into the stonework. “That’s the lock. It has to be opened with a key. The water doesn’t just push it open. Not like this. This was done deliberately.”

Nell felt a chill that had nothing to do with the evening air. “Who has a key?”

Kit’s expression was guarded. “Three people. Me. Tarrant. And the third one hangs on a hook in the mill office. It’s for the foreman, or in case of emergency. Any of us could have opened it.” He looked at her, his eyes searching hers. “But you don’t open it at midnight on a quiet night unless you mean to do harm. The river wasn’t high enough to warrant it. Tarrant was inspecting the race after he heard a noise. He went down there alone. Someone was waiting.”

He turned back to the gate, his profile stark against the rushing water. He looked tired, Nell thought, not just physically, but with a deep, bone-deep weariness. The town, she knew, whispered about him. A man who had served in the navy and come back changed. A man who held a key to a sluice gate. An easy suspect.

Nell looked from Kit’s weary face to the open gate, the water roaring its accusation into the quiet fenland evening. The constable’s simple explanation was dissolving, replaced by something darker and more complex. Ambrose Vane’s genial face, his offer of keys and help, flickered in her memory. And the ledgers, the neat columns of figures in the study. The Fen Drainage Board fund. Vane had been so keen to talk about it.

She straightened up, her decision made. This was no accident. Owen Tarrant had been pushed into that water, and someone in Lessing Fen was a murderer. And she, Nell Ashby, intended to find out who.
