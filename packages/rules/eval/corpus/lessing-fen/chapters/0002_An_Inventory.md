# Chapter 2: An_Inventory

The house of Owen Tarrant smelled of dust and grief. It was a substantial, brick-built house on the edge of the fen, with a view over the water meadows that now felt ominous. Nell stood in the hall, her notebook open, the list of assets from Mr. Pell a stark, practical thing in her hand. She was to list everything, from the Persian rug in the parlour to the teaspoons in the silver drawer.

She had not expected Ambrose Vane to be there. He had met her at the door with his usual warmth, his hand on her arm as if they were old friends. "Nell, my dear, you mustn't struggle with all this. Let me help you find your way about. Ivy is quite beside herself, of course. The poor lamb."

He followed her from room to room, pointing out pieces of furniture, offering anecdotes about Tarrant's taste in port wine, and generally making a nuisance of himself. He was helpful, of course, in his own way. He knew the house intimately, which was expected. But his hovering was a constant, low-pressure presence. He was a man who liked to be in control, and Nell felt the subtle weight of it as she moved from the drawing room to the study.

The study was a room of order and, Nell suspected, secrets. Bookshelves lined one wall, a large mahogany desk dominated the other. Ledgers, stacked in neat, precise piles, stood on a side table. Vane watched her as she ran a finger over the spines.

"Everything in here is for the bank, of course," he said, his voice smooth. "When Mr. Pell comes to sort the will, we'll need to present everything perfectly. The Fen Drainage Board accounts are particularly complex. I'll take the main ledgers now, shall I? Just to get them out of the way, keep them safe for the audit."

Before she could answer, he had lifted the entire stack of heavy, leather-bound books and was carrying them towards the door. Nell watched him go, a flicker of unease in her chest. She had not asked him to move them. She had been intending to go through them, methodically, page by page.

She turned her attention to the bookshelf. It held a different kind of ledger, smaller and more personal. These were Tarrant's own books, recording his private affairs. She ran her eye along the spines. There were ledgers for 1921, 1922, and the first two quarters of 1923. But the last quarter, the one covering the past three months, was missing. It should have been there, nestled between the others.

"Ambrose?" she called out, her voice sounding small in the quiet room.

He reappeared in the doorway, the box of ledgers under his arm. "Yes, Nell?"

"I was just looking at the shelf," she said, gesturing to the gap. "The ledger for the last quarter of '23 isn't here."

Vane’s genial expression didn’t flicker. He stepped into the room and peered at the shelf, as if seeing it for the first time. "Oh, that. I expect Owen must have taken it down to the mill. He was in a devil of a fuss about the drainage accounts last week. Said he wanted to cross-reference something with the sluice logs. Probably just left it on his desk there."

He gestured with his chin towards the large desk. It was a plausible enough explanation. Tarrant had been found at the mill. It made sense he would have papers with him. Nell nodded, though a small doubt remained. If it were on the desk, why wasn't it in plain sight? But she let it go. "Of course. Thank you, Ambrose."

He gave her another of his warm, reassuring smiles and left, the ledgers tucked safely under his arm. The house was suddenly, profoundly quiet. Nell stood in the middle of the study, the air thick with the scent of paper and polish. She walked over to the desk and looked, but it was clear, the surface wiped clean. The missing ledger was not there.

* * *

Later, she was in the hall, making a note on a small escritoire that stood beneath a large, dark oil painting of Tarrant. The painting showed a stern-faced man with heavy jowls, looking out at the viewer with an air of proprietary pride. Nell finished her note and turned to the coat rack, where Tarrant's good coat, a heavy tweed number, was hanging. She needed to check the pockets for any loose papers or personal effects.

Her hand went into the right pocket and found nothing. In the left, her fingers brushed against something small, hard, and cold. She pulled it out. It was a small silver whistle, attached to a thin, tarnished chain. It was old, its surface engraved with a name and a date: 'H.M.S. Cormorant 1916'.

The whistle was plain and functional, its metal worn smooth in places where it had been held. It was a bosun's whistle, the kind used for giving sharp, piercing commands on a ship. The engraving was clear, the letters precise. Nell turned it over in her palm, feeling its weight, its coolness. A nautical object in the house of a fenland miller who had never left the county. It was an anomaly, a piece that did not belong.

She held it up to the light from the window, studying it. Why would Owen Tarrant have a bosun's whistle from a naval vessel in 1916? He would have been in his forties then, certainly not of an age to be a junior sailor. The whistle felt significant, a small key to a lock she had not even known existed. It was a secret, a private thing. Ambrose Vane, hovering and helpful, would not have wanted her to find it. He would have wanted it for himself, or to have it disappear.

Nell closed her hand around the whistle, the silver pressing into her palm. She slipped it into her own coat pocket, the action deliberate and quiet. It was hers now. A secret to keep.

* * *

Supper at Number 12 Wick Street was a tense affair. The table was set, the aroma of roast beef and cabbage filling the small, comfortable parlour, but the usual easy chatter was absent. Aunt Maud bustled about, her face pinched with worry. Bea sat at the table, her chin held high, her eyes bright with a kind of defiant excitement.

Nell picked at her food, her mind still at Tarrant's house, on the missing ledger and the silver whistle. She was trying to fit the pieces together, but they refused to form a picture.

"It's all arranged," Bea announced, her voice a little too loud. She pushed her plate away. "Leonard has found a position in Halifax, with an importing firm. We sail from Liverpool in a month. Just a month!"

Nell looked up, her fork halfway to her mouth. "A month? Bea, you can't be serious."

"Why not? It's a new start. A new life." Bea's gaze was challenging.

"Because Leonard Fawley is in debt to half the shopkeepers in the county! Because he talks a fine game but has no substance to him!" The words were out before she could stop them, sharp and bitter.

Aunt Maud let out a small sob. "Nell, please. Don't quarrel."

"No, Maud, she's right to be concerned," Bea shot back, her own temper flaring. "You don't know Leonard like I do. He's ambitious. He's charming. He loves me."

"He loves the idea of you financing his escape from his creditors," Nell retorted. "You have no money, Bea. What are you going to live on? What are you going to eat?"

"We'll manage. We have to, don't we?" Bea's voice was trembling now. "You're just like everyone else. You see a man with a bit of spirit and you want to clip his wings. You want me to stay here and marry some dull clerk and live out my days in this stuffy little town."

"That's not true," Nell began, but Bea was on her feet.

"It is true! You're just afraid. Afraid of anything that's not predictable. Afraid of me being happy." She turned to their aunt. "I'm going to my room. Don't wait up."

She stormed from the room, the door slamming shut a moment later. The sound echoed in the sudden, heavy silence.

Aunt Maud dabbed at her eyes with her napkin. "Oh, Nell, what have you done? She's so excited. She thinks this is her great adventure."

Nell pushed her own chair back and stood, her appetite gone. "It's not an adventure, Maud, it's a folly. And I won't stand by and watch her walk into it."

"But what can you do? She's made up her mind." Aunt Maud's voice was weary, defeated.

Nell looked from her aunt's tear-streaked face to the closed door Bea had slammed. She thought of the silver whistle in her pocket, a secret from a dead man. She thought of the missing ledger, and the genial man who had carried it away. She thought of the roar of the mill race and Kit Merrow's tired, certain face.

There was a murderer in Lessing Fen. And here, in her own small, stuffy house, her own world felt as if it were coming apart at the seams. The two problems, she realised with a jolt of cold clarity, were one and the same. She could not solve one without solving the other. She had to find the truth about Owen Tarrant, and she had to find a way to save her sister. The weight of it settled on her shoulders, heavy and solid as the house itself.
