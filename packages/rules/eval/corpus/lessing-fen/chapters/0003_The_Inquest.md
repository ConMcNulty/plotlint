# Chapter 3: The_Inquest

The air in the Plough Inn’s best parlour was thick with pipe smoke and the scent of damp wool. A fire crackled in the grate, doing little to chase the chill from the room or the chill from the proceedings. Constable Dorrit sat ramrod straight in the witness box, his uniform straining slightly at the buttons. He spoke in a slow, deliberate monotone, recounting the events of the previous morning as if he were reading a parish notice. The coroner, a man named Mr. Withers from the next town, nodded along, his expression one of profound boredom.

"…and so, Mr. Tarrant, being alone and unobserved on the mill race, it is my professional opinion that he lost his footing on the wet stone," Dorrit concluded, his voice echoing slightly in the quiet room. "A tragic accident, pure and simple."

Mr. Withers made a note in his book. "No signs of a struggle, Constable?"

"None at all, sir. The body was found face down in the race. No defensive wounds. The gate was open, as it is every night for the drainage. A slip in the dark is all it takes."

Nell sat at the back of the room, her hands clenched in her lap. She wanted to stand up and shout. She wanted to ask why the sluice gate had been opened with a key, not by the usual mechanism. She wanted to ask about the missing ledger and the silver whistle she now felt in the pocket of her coat, a hard, cold secret. But she was just a clerk, and the law was a formal, closed shop. She was not permitted to speak.

Mr. Withers cleared his throat. "Are there any other witnesses to the discovery?"

Ambrose Vane, seated in the front row, rose smoothly. "I am, sir. I came to the mill early to prepare the books for the bank. I found the body, raised the alarm, and waited with the constable until help arrived."

"And where were you between midnight and the time you discovered the body, Mr. Vane?"

"At home, sir. In bed. I can't imagine what use that is, but it is the truth." He was perfectly composed, his face a mask of sorrow. He looked every inch the grieving junior partner.

Mr. Withers seemed satisfied. He thanked the witnesses and pronounced the death of Owen Tarrant to be accidental, caused by misadventure. The room emptied with a collective sigh of relief, the business concluded, the matter closed.

* * *

Nell waited, letting the crowd thin before she made her way down the narrow, creaking stairs. Ambrose Vane was just ahead of her, descending with a careful, unhurried grace. As he reached the bottom step, he turned to offer her a commiserating smile. "A dreadful business, Miss Ashby. I hope it wasn't too distressing for you to sit through."

"It was necessary, Mr. Vane," she replied, her voice steady.

He nodded, his genial expression unwavering. "Of course. Necessary for the estate." He took a step towards the inn door, and as he did, Nell’s gaze fell to his feet. His highly polished black boots were immaculate, except for one detail. In the deep welts where the leather met the sole, there was a caking of dried, greyish-brown clay. It was the specific, heavy, peaty mud of the fen bank, the very soil one would find after walking along the sluice path in the damp night air. He had told the coroner he was at home in bed. The mud told a different story.

Her eyes snapped up to his face. He saw her look. A flicker of something— amusement? challenge?—crossed his features before he smoothed it away into a look of mild concern. He smiled again, a perfect, practised curve of the lips. "Clay from the yard, I'm afraid. The walk from the mill is treacherous after the rain."

He gave a slight, dismissive shrug and walked out of the inn, leaving Nell alone on the stairs. The clump of his boots on the flagged floor was the only sound. She stood there for a long moment, the image of the mud burned into her mind. It was a mistake, a careless slip in his carefully constructed performance. And it was her first real piece of evidence.

* * *

The inn yard was cold and damp, smelling of wet straw and woodsmoke. A small group of men from the market were huddled near the pump, their voices low but carrying on the still air.

"...can't be an accident, not with the sluice open like that," one man was saying, his face earnest. "It's the only way in."

"Who else has a key, then?" another grunted. "Only Tarrant, and he's gone. And that Merrow fellow at the lock."

"Ah, there's the one," a third man chimed in, nodding sagely. "Keeps to himself, that one. Navy man, they say. Quiet as a mouse. Why would a man like that want to stay in a place like Lessing Fen? Got something to hide, mark my words."

Nell felt a hot spike of anger. She didn't know Kit Merrow well, but she knew he was no murderer. She stepped forward, her voice sharper than she intended. "That's not fair. Mr. Merrow was on duty all night. He has an alibi."

The men turned to look at her, surprised. The first man, a burly mill-hand named Sam, frowned. "He's got a key, though. Anyone with a key could have done it."

"Not anyone," Nell said, meeting his gaze. "The key opens the gate from the top, by the office. You'd have to be there. And Mr. Merrow was at the lock. He was seen."

She didn't add that he had told her the gate was opened deliberately, a fact that would brand him as a fool in their eyes. She just needed to plant a seed of doubt. The men exchanged uneasy looks and mumbled amongst themselves, the conversation dying away.

As she turned to leave, a quiet voice spoke from her elbow. "Miss Ashby?"

She looked up into Kit Merrow’s tired, watchful face. He had been leaning against the wall of the inn, unseen, his cap pulled low. "Thank you," he said, the words seeming to cost him an effort. "For that."

"It was the truth, Mr. Merrow," she said, her own voice softening.

He gave a short, humourless nod. "The truth is often a lonely thing." He gestured with his head towards the bridge over the river. "Walk with me? It's a better road than the one they're digging up near the lock."

They walked in silence for a moment, the sound of their footsteps the only company. The river, sluggish and brown, flowed past them. "They're all talking about me," Kit said, his voice low. "It's what they do. A stranger in a small town. A man with a key."

"I know," Nell said. "They're frightened. It's easier to blame a quiet man than to admit someone they know might be capable of it."

He shot her a sideways glance, surprised. "You don't believe it was an accident, then."

"No," she said simply. "I don't."

They reached the bridge and stopped, leaning on the cold stone parapet. The mill race was visible downstream, the sluice gate a dark, gaping mouth. "He had it coming," Kit said, his voice flat. "Tarrant. He was a hard man. Made life difficult for a lot of people. Drainage Board rates, mill prices... he squeezed everyone dry."

"Did you have a reason to wish him harm, Mr. Merrow?"

He gave a short, bitter laugh. "No more than half the town. But I didn't push him in the race. I was at the lock, tending to the water levels, as I do every night. Mr. Pell can confirm it. He wrote a letter for me after ten. I was in my hut."

Nell believed him. There was a solid, unshakeable certainty about him that matched her own. "Why did you tell me the gate was opened with a key?" she asked.

"Because it was," he said, his gaze fixed on the mill. "It wasn't set for the morning flush. It was opened manually. With a key. Someone wanted that water flowing fast last night. Someone wanted to make sure the race was dangerous."

He fell silent, and Nell thought of the mud on Ambrose Vane's boots. The genial bookkeeper, offering tea and keys, the first person to find the body. The man who had told the coroner he was safe in his bed. The pieces were fitting together, a slow, grim picture forming in her mind.

"Thank you, Mr. Merrow," she said. "For telling me."

He gave another curt nod. "Be careful, Miss Ashby. Asking questions in Lessing Fen can be as dangerous as walking on a wet sluice gate in the dark."

He touched his cap and walked away, leaving her alone on the bridge. The wind picked up, whipping her hair across her face. She looked from the mill, where the murderer worked in plain sight, to the town, where her sister was planning to ruin her life with a charming gambler. The two problems, the murder and Bea's folly, were no longer separate. They were a tangle, a single, dangerous knot, and she was the only one who seemed to see it. And she had no idea how to begin to untie it.
