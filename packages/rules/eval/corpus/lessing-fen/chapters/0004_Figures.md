# Chapter 4: Figures

Nell walked into the Fen & County Bank, the bell above the door giving a cheerful little tinkle that seemed at odds with the grim thoughts in her head. The air inside smelled of paper, polish, and a faint, sweetish scent from the vase of freesias on the manager's desk. Mr. Hollis, a small, bald man with spectacles perched on the end of his nose, looked up from his ledger, his smile polite but distant.

"Miss Ashby," he said. "To do with the Tarrant estate, I presume? Mr. Pell wrote."

"Yes, Mr. Hollis. I'm beginning the inventory." She sat down opposite him, her hands folded in her lap. "I understand Mr. Tarrant had a number of accounts with you."

"Indeed," Hollis said, pushing his spectacles up his nose. "The mill account, of course, and a personal one for Mr. and Mrs. Tarrant. And then there was the Fen Drainage Board account. A trust fund, managed by the mill. Quite a substantial sum, for the upkeep of the local drainage system."

"I see," Nell said, though she didn't, not entirely. "I'll need to see the relevant ledgers."

"Certainly, certainly. They'll be in the strong room." Hollis rose and gestured for her to follow him. As he unlocked a heavy door, he chattered on. "A very responsible position, managing that fund. Mr. Tarrant took it very seriously. In fact, it was a matter of some discussion just recently."

"Oh?" Nell asked, keeping her voice neutral.

"A week ago last Tuesday," Hollis said, leading her down a short corridor. "Mr. Tarrant came in to see me. He asked for a full audit of the mill's accounts. Specifically, he mentioned the Fen Drainage Board fund. Said he wanted to ensure everything was in order before the end of the financial year. He seemed… well, he seemed rather agitated about it."

He stopped outside another door, this one made of steel. "He said he'd noticed a discrepancy, a small one, but he wanted it looked into thoroughly." He unlocked the door and gestured her inside. The strong room was cool and smelled of dust and metal. Rows of ledgers stood on shelves, their leather spines cracked.

"A discrepancy?" Nell repeated, her heart giving a small, hard thump against her ribs.

"Yes," Hollis said, pulling a ledger from a shelf. "He didn't elaborate. Just said he needed the bank's eyes on it. A perfectly reasonable request, of course. But the timing was notable. The very next day, he changed his will, dissolving the partnership with his junior man."

He handed her the ledger. It was the mill's main account book. Nell opened it, her fingers feeling clumsy. She turned to the last page of the previous quarter. The figures were dense columns of debits and credits, a language she understood well. She scanned them, looking for anything that might connect to the Drainage Board fund. Hollis stood by, watching her with an expectant air.

Nell closed the ledger and handed it back. "Thank you, Mr. Hollis. That's very helpful."

He smiled, relieved to have been of service. "Not at all. If there's anything else…"

"No, that's all for now." Nell left the bank, the bell jangling behind her. The discrepancy. Tarrant had found something. He had asked for an audit. He had changed his will. And then he had drowned. Ambrose Vane, the genial bookkeeper who had hovered over her during the inventory, had mentioned he needed the ledgers 'for the bank'. The missing quarter's ledger. The mud on his boots. The pieces clicked into place with a sound as sharp and final as the slam of a cell door. Tarrant hadn't slipped. He had been silenced before he could expose the theft.

* * *

The house on Wick Street was thick with the smell of dust and Bea's lavender water. Bea stood in the middle of her bedroom, a pile of dresses on the bed, her cheeks flushed with excitement. On the floor, a large, half-empty trunk stood open.

"It's all going so well," she said, holding up a silk scarf to the light. "Leonand says Liverpool is a magnificent city. All the ships, and the docks, and the American liners. He says it's the gateway to the world."

Nell leaned against the doorframe, her arms crossed. "Bea, please. Listen to me. We need to talk about this."

"There's nothing to talk about," Bea said, folding the scarf with unnecessary care. "I'm going. I'm going with Leonard."

"You barely know him," Nell said, her voice tight. "And you know the rumours. The card debts, the money he owes to Mr. Pell, to the tailor in Spalding…"

"He's a charming man," Bea said, her chin lifting. "You're just jealous because you're stuck here in Lessing Fen, with your ledgers and your solicitor's ink. You don't want me to have any happiness."

"Don't be absurd," Nell snapped, stepping into the room. "I'm not jealous. I'm worried. This isn't a romantic adventure, it's a fool's errand. He'll spend what little money you have in a week, and then what will you do? Alone in a foreign country?"

"I'll be with Leonard," Bea said, but there was a flicker of uncertainty in her eyes. "He'll take care of me."

"He says he'll take care of you," Nell corrected her. "That's not the same thing. He's a gambler, Bea. A charming one, I'm sure, but a gambler nonetheless. Gamblers don't take care of people; they take from them."

"You don't know him!" Bea's voice rose. "You just see what you want to see. You always have. You and Aunt Maud, with your sensible heads and your sensible lives. Well, I'm not sensible. I want to live!"

She threw the scarf into the trunk with a dramatic gesture. "You can't stop me, Nell. My ticket is bought. My passage is paid for. I'm leaving in a month."

Nell opened her mouth to argue, to lay out every damning piece of information she had about Leonard Fawley, but the words caught in her throat. Bea was a grown woman. Stubborn, headstrong, and blind to Leonard's faults, but grown. She would not be persuaded. Nell felt a familiar, weary despair settle over her. The murder, the danger, the secrets she was keeping—it all felt too much. And now this. Her sister, sailing towards what was almost certainly a ruinous future.

She closed her mouth, the fight draining out of her. "Just… be careful, Bea," she said, her voice soft. "Please."

Bea looked at her, and for a moment, the old sisterly affection seemed to surface. "I will," she said quietly. "And you… you should try to be happy for me."

Nell couldn't answer. She just turned and walked out, closing the door on the scent of lavender and a future she was powerless to change.

* * *

The wind off the fens was sharp, carrying the damp, earthy smell of the water. Nell walked along the towpath towards the lock, her hands thrust deep into her coat pockets. She needed to see Kit. The bank had confirmed it. The motive was there, clear as mud on a boot. Now she needed to know about the keys.

Kit's hut was a small, grey stone building perched on the bank of the river. A thin curl of smoke rose from the chimney. As she approached, the door opened and Kit stepped out, wiping his hands on a rag. He saw her and stopped, his expression guarded.

"Miss Ashby," he said. "Is something wrong?"

"No," she said, climbing the few stone steps to the door. "Not wrong. I just… I need to ask you something. About the keys."

He gestured for her to enter. The inside of the hut was small, neat, and smelled of coal smoke and tar. A kettle hissed on a small stove, and a wooden rack on the wall held various tools. On the wall by the door was a wooden board with three brass hooks.

"The key board," Kit said, following her gaze. He pointed to the middle hook. "That's mine. The sluice key. It hasn't left that nail since I took the job."

Nell leaned closer. The key was a heavy, brass thing, its teeth worn smooth. It was exactly where he said it would be.

"And the third one," she said. "The one from the mill office. You said it was gone the morning after… after the death."

"It was," Kit said, his voice flat. "I checked. The hook was empty. Tarrant's key was usually hanging there. He never took it off, not even to sleep."

"So," Nell said, turning to face him, "the killer had access to the mill office after hours. They took the key, opened the sluice, and put it back before anyone was the wiser."

Kit nodded. "That's the only way. Unless they had a copy made."

"A copy," Nell mused. "Who could make a copy? Who has access to the office?"

Kit looked at her, his eyes dark and serious. "Vane. He's the bookkeeper. He's in and out of that office all day long. He knows where everything is. He could have made a copy of the key any time he liked."

Nell thought of Ambrose Vane, his warm smile, his offer of tea, his helpful hovering. The man who had carried off the ledgers, who had told the coroner he was in his bed. The man with the mud on his boots.

"He was the first one to find the body," she said, more to herself than to him. "He said he came to check the mill before work, found the gate open, and saw Tarrant in the race. He pulled him out."

"Convenient," Kit said, his voice a low rumble. "Very convenient."

They stood in silence for a moment, the only sound the hiss of the kettle. The wind whistled around the eaves of the hut.

"So we know who had the opportunity," Nell said, her mind working. "We know he had a reason. Tarrant was about to ruin him. But we need proof. We need to know for sure."

Kit picked up a rag from the table and began to polish a brass fitting, his movements slow and deliberate. He didn't look at her. "And how do you propose we get proof, Miss Ashby? Vane's a clever man. He's not going to leave his name written on the wall."

Nell looked from the key on the wall to Kit's set face. She had a partner now, a man who understood the danger and was willing to stand with her on the edge of the water. The knot of her problems, the murder and her sister's flight, seemed a little less tangled, a little less impossible to face.

"We start with the ledgers," she said. "He took them from the house for 'safekeeping'. The bank said Tarrant asked for an audit of the Drainage Board fund. I need to see that ledger, Mr. Merrow. I need to see what Vane was so desperate to hide."

Kit stopped polishing. He looked at her, and in his eyes, Nell saw a flicker of the same grim determination she felt herself. He gave a single, sharp nod.

"Then that's where we'll start," he said. "The mill office. We'll see what the books have to say."
