# Chapter 5: The_Cell

Constable Dorrit was polishing his already-polishable helmet when Nell walked into the small, cheerless office behind the police house. A fire smouldered in the hearth, doing little to chase the damp from the air. Dorrit looked up, his face a mask of weary resignation.

"Ashby. Come to report a lost cat, have you?" He put the helmet down with a clatter. "Or is it a missing teacup?"

"I'm here about Mr. Tarrant's death, Constable," Nell said, closing the door behind her. Dorrit winced as if the word had a physical weight. "I believe it was murder, and I think I know who did it."

Dorrit sighed, a long, drawn-out sound like air escaping a punctured tyre. He gestured to a wooden chair opposite his desk. "Sit down, Miss Ashby. You've been listening to too many of Mrs. Gable's tales at the post office. The coroner has spoken. It was an accident. Slippery bank, dark night, tragic end. All very sad."

"It wasn't an accident," Nell insisted, taking the seat. She kept her voice level, though her heart was beating a little too fast. "The sluice gate was opened deliberately. With a key. Kit Merrow told me that himself. And I have reason to believe Ambrose Vane was the one who had the third key."

Dorrit's brow furrowed. "Ambrose Vane? Now, you can't be serious. The poor man was Tarrant's partner. Devastated. Brought us all cups of tea at the inquest."

"He was also desperate," Nell pressed on. "The bank manager, Mr. Hollis, told me Tarrant was about to demand a full audit of the Fen Drainage Board accounts the mill manages. Mr. Vane is the bookkeeper. If there were... irregularities... he would be ruined. And I saw something myself. After the inquest, I noticed his boots. They had grey fen clay caked in the welts. The same kind of clay you find on the bank by the sluice gate. He told the coroner he was at home all night."

Dorrit stared at her, his small eyes unblinking. He picked up a pencil and tapped it on a blotter. "Clay on boots? In this county, Miss Ashby, you could find fen clay on the boots of the bishop if he'd been to the market. It proves nothing. And Vane? A pillar of the community. Gives to the flower fund, organises the whist drives. You're chasing shadows."

"I'm not," Nell said, her stubbornness hardening into resolve. "And there's more. He took the mill ledgers from Tarrant's house for 'safekeeping'. The very ledgers that would show the Drainage Board accounts. He's hiding them."

Dorrit put the pencil down with a thud. "Look here, Miss Ashby, I know you mean well. But you're a clerk, not a policeman. You poke around in things that don't concern you, and it only causes trouble. The coroner has closed the case. I have a town to police, not to listen to fanciful theories about a pillar like Ambrose Vane."

"But what about Kit Merrow?" Nell countered. "He's the one being suspected by everyone. He's quiet, he lives by himself, he has a key. It's not fair."

Dorrit leaned back in his creaking chair, a flicker of something like satisfaction in his eyes. "Fairness has little to do with it, Miss Ashby. Public peace does. The gossips are saying Merrow did it. He has the key. He's an outsider. It makes a simple story for simple people. A story they can understand."

"That's not justice!"

"Justice is a slow and cumbersome business, Miss Ashby," Dorrit said, his tone suddenly decisive. "And in the meantime, I have to keep the peace. So, to give the gossips what they want, and to stop you from causing a panic, I've taken Merrow into custody. For his own protection, you understand."

Nell stared at him, speechless. "You've arrested him?"

"On suspicion," Dorrit said, picking up his helmet and giving it a final buff. "His key fits the gate. It's a start. Now, if you'll excuse me, I have a cell to guard. And a lot of paperwork to fill out." He stood up, a clear dismissal.

Nell left the police house, the cold air outside a shock. Dorrit hadn't just ignored her; he had used her own words against her, locking up an innocent man to quiet the town. The injustice of it settled in her stomach like a stone. She had led them straight to Kit.

* * *

The sky was the colour of a dirty dishcloth as Nell made her way to the station to catch the early train to Liverpool. She hadn't slept, the image of Kit Merrow in a cold cell playing over and over in her mind. Bea was already waiting on the platform, a small, valise at her feet, her face pale and resolute.

"You're up early," Bea said, her voice tight.

"So are you," Nell replied. "I came to see you off. Are you sure about this, Bea? Leonard's story doesn't add up. The debts, the urgency... it's all too convenient."

Bea's chin lifted. "You don't know him. You're just jealous because I'm leaving this dull town and you're not."

"I'm not jealous, I'm worried," Nell said, keeping her voice low. "Worried that you're throwing your life away on a man who sees you as a solution to his problems."

"I see you as a problem, Nell," Bea said, her voice cracking. "You always have to be the sensible one, the worrier. Well, I'm not you. I'm going to Canada. I'm going to have a life." She looked past Nell, her gaze fixed on the track.

The train hissed into the platform, steam billowing in the damp air. Bea turned and embraced Nell briefly, her body stiff. "I'll write from the ship," she said, pulling away. "Don't worry."

The whistle blew. Bea picked up her valise and climbed the steps without a backward glance. Nell watched her find a window and sit down, her face turned to the glass. The train jerked forward, gathering speed, and then it was gone, swallowed by the grey expanse of the fens. Nell stood on the empty platform, the wind whipping her hair across her face. The knot of her problems felt tighter than ever. She had failed to save her sister, and she had helped send an innocent man to jail. The weight of it was immense.

* * *

The cell was small and smelled of damp stone and something faintly, unpleasantly sour. Kit Merrow sat on the narrow cot, his back against the wall, his eyes closed. He opened them when Nell approached the heavy iron bars. He didn't look like a prisoner; he looked like a man who had simply retreated into himself.

"Miss Ashby," he said, his voice flat. "Come to gloat?"

"Of course not," Nell said, her voice barely a whisper. "I came to apologise. I told Constable Dorbit about my suspicions. I didn't realise... I never imagined he would arrest you."

Kit gave a short, humourless laugh. "He had to. It's the easiest thing to do. Blame the quiet outsider with the key. It saves him from having to do any real work." He stood up and walked to the bars, his face pale in the dim light filtering through the high, barred window. "You shouldn't have come. It only makes it look worse."

"I had to," Nell insisted. "I had to see you. And I need you to tell me everything. Everything you remember about the night Mr. Tarrant died."

Kit hesitated, his gaze fixed on something over her shoulder. "There's nothing to tell. I was in my hut. I went to bed. I heard nothing."

"Nonsense," Nell said, her voice gaining strength. "You told me before. You said you saw a lantern."

Kit's shoulders slumped. "I saw a light. Moving along the sluice bank, heading for the mill office. It was late. Past midnight. I assumed it was Mr. Tarrant, going to check on the water levels. It happens. I thought nothing of it."

Nell's heart hammered against her ribs. "You saw it? And you didn't think it was strange?"

"The sluice bank isn't exactly a well-lit thoroughfare, Miss Ashby," Kit said, a touch of his old wryness returning. "People do walk there at night. I saw a light, I assumed it was him, I went back to sleep. End of story."

"It's not the end of the story," Nell said, gripping the cold iron bars. "It's the beginning. Who else would be out there at that hour, with a lantern, heading for the mill office? Someone who knew Tarrant was going to be there. Someone who had a key."

Kit looked at her, his eyes dark and serious. "You're not going to let this go, are you?"

"No," Nell said. "I'm not. You're being punished for something you didn't do, and a murderer is free. I can't let that happen."

Kit was silent for a long moment. He looked back into the gloom of his cell, then back at her. The spark of grim determination she had seen in his eyes at the lock was there again, but it was tempered now with a weary resignation. "Then you're a fool," he said, his voice low and urgent. "A stubborn, interfering fool. Leave it alone, Miss Ashby. Go back to your ledgers and your sister's Canadian adventure. Let Dorrit have his easy answer. It's safer for everyone. Especially for you."

He turned away from the bars, his back to her. Nell stood there, the cold of the iron seeping into her hands, the echo of his warning hanging in the air. She had a partner, but he was telling her to walk away. The murder, the ledger, the whistle in her pocket, and now this. The knot of her problems felt impossible. But looking at the set of Kit Merrow's shoulders, Nell knew she couldn't. She wouldn't.
