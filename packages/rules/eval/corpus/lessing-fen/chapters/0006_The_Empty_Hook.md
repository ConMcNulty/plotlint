# Chapter 6: The_Empty_Hook

The mill office smelled of old paper, damp wool, and the faint, sweet tang of the tea leaves Ambrose Vane had just brewed. He had met Nell at the door with a ready smile, his hands outstretched as if to guide her over a threshold of dust and sorrow. "Miss Ashby, do come in. It's a grim place, I know, but I've put the kettle on. A hot cup will make it bearable, I'm sure."

The office was a long, low room with a high counter that ran along one wall, stained with ink and ringed with dark circles from countless cups of tea. On shelves above, the mill's great ledgers were bound in cracked leather, their spines thick with the accumulated business of seasons. Nell had already seen these at Tarrant's house, but here they were the working copies, their pages smudged with calculations and notes in Tarrant's sprawling hand. Ambrose bustled about, finding a clean cup and pouring the tea, his movements precise and economical. He was a man who liked things orderly, a fact reflected in the neat stacks of paper on his own desk, the polished brass of the ledger clasp, the absence of a single speck of dust on the windowsill.

Nell sipped her tea, the warmth a small comfort in the chill of the room. She had come here to see the office itself, the space from which the mill was run, a place the coroner and the constable had paid little mind. Ambrose, of course, had offered to accompany her. He was, after all, the junior partner, the man who knew every inch of the business.

"Thank you, Mr. Vane," Nell said, her eyes scanning the room. "I just wanted to get the measure of the place for the inventory. It's all part of the process."

"Of course, of course," he said, leaning against the counter opposite her, his teacup held loosely in his hands. He watched her with an expression of helpful concern. "Let me know if you need anything lifted down or any of the records explained. I'm at your service."

Nell walked slowly around the perimeter of the room, her gaze taking in everything. The worn chair where Tarrant must have sat, the blotter on the desk, the safe in the corner. She tried to imagine Owen Tarrant here, late at night, the only light the pool from a single lamp. Her eyes kept returning to the wall by the door, to a small, empty brass hook screwed into the wood, just above a small wooden bench. It was a simple hook, nothing special, but its emptiness seemed significant.

"Mr. Vane," she said, her voice casual. "I was told there's a sluice key kept here. On that hook."

Ambrose followed her gaze. He gave a soft, dismissive sigh, a puff of air that seemed to carry the weight of minor inconvenience. "Ah, yes. That old thing. We haven't seen it for months, have we? Owen was always meaning to get a new one cut, but it was never at the top of his list. Must have been mislaid somewhere. A shame, really. It would have made things easier last week when the gate stuck."

Nell felt a prickle of something, a confirmation of a suspicion she had not been able to voice until now. "So it's been gone for some time?"

"Oh, I shouldn't think it was *gone*," Ambrose said, picking up a pencil and tapping it thoughtfully against his teeth. "More likely misplaced. You know Owen. He had a dozen projects on the go at once. Probably took it down to use it somewhere else and simply forgot to put it back. These things happen." He smiled, a warm, placid expression that was meant to put her at ease. "It's a small thing, I'm sure. Of no consequence now."

But it was of consequence. The hook was empty. The key was missing. And Ambrose Vane, the genial bookkeeper, was telling her it had been lost for months, a simple oversight. She looked at him, at his neat, pressed waistcoat and the calm certainty in his eyes, and felt a cold knot tighten in her stomach. It was too neat, too convenient. A story designed to explain away a hole in the facts.

She nodded slowly, not letting her own thoughts show on her face. "I see. Well, thank you for clarifying, Mr. Vane. I'll just make a note of it for the inventory."

* * *

The Tarrant house on the High Street was a monument to grief. Ivy Tarrant received Nell in the small front parlour, a room that smelled of lavender polish and unshed tears. She sat upright in a stiff-backed chair, her hands folded in her lap, her face a pale mask of brittle composure. Any hint of the warmth Nell had heard about in the town's descriptions of the mill owner's wife was buried deep beneath a shroud of shock and suspicion.

"Miss Ashby," Ivy said, her voice thin and reedy. "Mr. Pell's clerk. What can I do for you? My solicitor told me you are going through Owen's papers. I hope you find what you need." Her eyes were red-rimmed but dry, and she would not meet Nell's gaze for more than a second at a time.

Nell had come with the excuse of discussing the disposition of some personal items, but she had another purpose. "I'm very sorry for your loss, Mrs. Tarrant," she began gently. "It must be a terrible thing to lose a husband so suddenly. It must make you wonder about... well, about what was on his mind in his final days."

Ivy Tarrant's head came up a fraction, her knuckles whitening where her fingers pressed together. "Wonder? About what?"

Nell chose her words carefully. "The coroner called it an accident, of course. But people talk. They say Mr. Tarrant was a man of business, with many concerns. Was there anything... anything at all... that was troubling him in the week before he died? A disagreement, perhaps? A worry about the mill?"

Ivy stared at a point just over Nell's shoulder, her gaze distant. "Disagreements," she said, her voice flat. "Owen had a temper. He was a good man, but he was stubborn. He argued with the baker about the price of flour. He argued with the blacksmith over a bill for new scythes. Men argue. It's what men do."

"But was there anything more serious?" Nell pressed. "Something that might have upset him deeply?"

A flicker of something—anger, perhaps—crossed Ivy's face, gone as quickly as it appeared. "There was the Drainage money," she said, the words clipped and sharp. "That was the big one. Owen was always fussing over it. Said the books had to be absolutely perfect. He had a new man from the bank coming in to look at it. That's all I know. He was in a foul mood about it for days."

Drainage money. The phrase hung in the air. Nell filed it away, a confirmation of the slip Mr. Hollis had made at the bank. It was the key, she felt sure of it. The money that had been stolen.

"And was there anyone he argued with about this?" Nell asked softly. "About the Drainage Board?"

Ivy's eyes, for the first time, held a direct, hard look. "There was my brother," she said. "Silas. He came here on the Tuesday. The shouting could be heard in the street. Owen was furious. Silas... Silas has always been a fool with money. He's in debt up to his neck. He'd borrowed a considerable sum from Owen, and it seems he'd lost it all at cards. He wanted more. Owen refused. It was a dreadful scene. He left here white with rage."

Nell felt a jolt. Silas Rudd. The gambler. The red herring Constable Dorrit was surely chasing right now. But Ivy's suspicion was palpable. She believed her brother was capable of violence. Or perhaps she was trying to point Nell away from someone else. She looked at the grieving widow, so rigid in her grief, and saw not just a woman mourning her husband, but a woman guarding a secret, or terrified of one.

"I'm sorry, Mrs. Tarrant," Nell said, standing up. "I shouldn't have troubled you with such questions."

"No," Ivy said, her voice regaining its thin quality. "It's... it's good to talk about it. To remember what he was like." She looked down at her hands again. "If you find out... anything, Miss Ashby... about what truly happened..."

Nell hesitated, then gave a small, non-committal nod. "I'll do my best."

* * *

The sky had turned the colour of a bruised plum by the time Nell walked back towards Wick Street. The air was damp and cool, carrying the reedy scent of the fenlands as dusk settled. She was thinking of Ivy Tarrant's pale face and her whispered accusations of her brother, and of Ambrose Vane's easy smile and his lie about the empty hook. The pieces were shifting, forming a shape she did not yet understand.

A figure stepped out from under the archway of the bridge, and her heart gave a small jump. It was Kit Merrow. He was out of his cell, released that afternoon for lack of evidence. He looked different without the constraint of his uniform, in a simple tweed jacket and trousers, but the same weary reserve was in his eyes. He gave her a curt nod.

"Dorrit let you out," Nell said, more a statement than a question.

"He had no choice," Kit replied, his voice low. "No witnesses. No proof. Just gossip." He fell into step beside her as she continued along the towpath. The path was narrow, the river a dark, silent ribbon beside them. "What are you doing out this late?"

"Visiting Mrs. Tarrant," Nell said. "And the mill office before that."

Kit didn't ask for details. He just walked, his hands shoved deep into his pockets, his gaze fixed on the water. The silence between them was comfortable, not strained. They had crossed a line in the police cell, a line of shared knowledge and purpose. He might tell her to leave it alone, but she knew he wouldn't. Not really.

"You shouldn't be talking to me," he said suddenly, his voice rough. "It's not good for you. Dorrit will be watching. Vane will be watching."

"I can't help it," Nell said. "And neither can you. We both know it wasn't an accident."

He let out a short, humourless sigh. "No. It wasn't." He stopped walking and turned to face her, the last of the daylight catching the line of his jaw. "I saw something that night. I told you in the cell. A lantern. Moving along the bank towards the mill. I thought it was Tarrant. But what if it wasn't? What if it was the person who had the key? The person who opened that gate."

"Someone from the mill," Nell murmured. "Someone who knew Tarrant's routine. Who knew he was going to be down there."

Kit nodded. "And who had a reason. The Drainage money. That's what this is about, isn't it? Tarrant found out he was stealing, and he was going to expose him."

The directness of his accusation, the simple logic of it, made Nell catch her breath. He was saying what she had only suspected, what the clues were pointing towards. She looked at him, at the intensity in his eyes, and felt a pull that was more than just shared determination. It was a connection, a spark in the gathering dark.

They had reached the gate of her aunt's house, the warm yellow light from the window spilling out onto the path. The moment stretched, fragile and charged. Kit didn't move. He just looked at her, his gaze searching her face.

"Miss Ashby," he began, his voice softer now. "Nell..."

He didn't finish. He took a small step forward, closing the distance between them. Nell felt the pull of it, an instinct to meet him halfway, to bridge the space that had suddenly become too wide. She could see the question in his eyes, a silent plea that was both an invitation and a warning. She could feel the cold of the evening air on her skin, and the warmth of his presence so close to her own.

Then, from the open window behind her, Aunt Maud's voice called out, "Nell, dear? Is that you? Your tea's getting cold."

The spell was broken. Kit stepped back, the moment of intimacy dissolving into the awkwardness of a near-miss. He gave her a final, unreadable look, a mixture of frustration and something else she couldn't name, then turned and walked away without another word, disappearing back towards the bridge and the river.

Nell stood for a moment on her own doorstep, the echo of his name unsaid on her lips, the cold night air suddenly feeling very lonely. She had a partner in this, but she was more alone than ever.
