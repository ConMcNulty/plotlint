# Chapter 7: What_the_Reeds_Kept

The first grey light of dawn was bleeding into the sky, turning the reeds along the bank into a wall of black lace against the paleness. Kit Merrow stood by the lock, the familiar weight of the windlass in his hand, the groan of the timbers a comfort he hadn't known he needed. The air was damp and cold, smelling of mud and decay, but it was clean. It was honest. Unlike the thoughts that had been churning in his head since he’d left Nell on her doorstep.

He’d almost kissed her. The memory was a physical thing, a warmth in his chest that warmed him even now in the pre-d chill. He saw her face in the gloom, the way her eyes had held his, the way she’d leaned in, just a fraction. And then her aunt’s voice, slicing through the moment like a knife. He’d stepped back, the spell broken, leaving behind a frustration that was sharper than any reprimand from his old commander.

He couldn't afford to be distracted. Not now. Not with the shadow of Tarrant’s death hanging over him, however unjustly. He had to focus. He had to be the quiet, reliable lock-keeper the town thought he was. He turned his attention to the sluice gate, the great wooden teeth that held back the river. He began the slow, methodical turn of the windlass, the mechanism groaning in protest. Below, the mill race began to stir, its dark waters swirling and churning as the level dropped.

As the water receded, the bank below the gate became exposed. Kit’s gaze, accustomed to searching for debris or damage, snagged on a clump of reeds that seemed unnaturally dense. He stopped turning the windlass, peering closer. They weren't just reeds. Something was tucked into the reedy fringe, half-submerged in the mud at the water’s edge. He let the gate settle, the water stilling, and made his way down the slippery bank.

The mud sucked at his boots as he approached. He crouched, his fingers plunging into the cold slime, and pulled free a heavy, brass-bound object. It was a storm lantern, its glass chimney thick with grime, its brass casing stamped with a single, clear word: 'TARRANT MILL OFFICE'.

Kit’s heart gave a dull thud. Not Tarrant’s house. The mill office. So the light he’d seen moving along the bank hadn’t come from the direction of Tarrant’s home. It had come from the office. And it had been hidden here, in the reeds, after being used. He wiped the mud from the side with his thumb, the metal cold and smooth under his touch. He knew this lantern. He’d seen it on the hook in the mill office, next to the ledger books. It was the one Ambrose Vane was always polishing, always offering to light for visitors.

A fresh wave of unease washed over him. This was a piece of the puzzle. A cold, hard piece. And it had been hidden. Who would hide a lantern? Who would use it to walk along the sluice bank in the dead of night? He looked from the lantern back towards the lock-keeper’s hut, a small, sturdy building with smoke curling from its chimney. He had a decision to make. He could hide it, wait, see what developed. Or he could take it to Nell. The thought of her face, her focus, made his decision for him. He tucked the lantern under his arm, its weight a solid, promising thing, and began the walk to the town.

* * *

The bell above the door of Mr. Pell’s office gave a tired jingle as Kit pushed it open. The room smelled of old paper, dust, and the faint, sweet scent of Mr. Pell’s pipe tobacco. Nell was sitting at a large, cluttered desk, her head bent over a sheaf of papers, a fountain pen held poised over a line of figures. She looked up, and her expression shifted from concentration to surprise, then to a dawning, bright curiosity.

"Mr. Merrow," she said, standing up. "What can I do for you?"

"I found this," he said, holding the lantern out towards her. "Below the sluice. In the reeds."

Nell took it from him, her fingers brushing his. The contact was brief, but it sent a small current through him. She turned the lantern over, her eyes scanning the brass stamp. "Tarrant Mill Office," she murmured. "So it is. Why would it be there?"

"Because it wasn't a flood that opened the gate," Kit said, his voice low. "Someone used a key. And someone used this lantern to see what they were doing. The light I saw… it came from this."

Nell looked at the lantern, then back at him. The realisation seemed to dawn on her, slow and certain. "You saw it from the lock," she breathed. "You saw the lantern on the sluice bank. You thought it was Tarrant, but it wasn't. It was whoever took the key from the office hook."

Kit nodded. "The key Vane said was lost for months. The one he said Owen kept meaning to replace." The pieces were fitting together, forming a shape that was ugly and sharp. "The light came from the office, Nell. Not the house. It changes things."

He watched her face as she processed it. There was a fire in her eyes now, a fierce intelligence that he found mesmerising. She wasn't just a clerk; she was a solver of puzzles. And she was looking at him as if he’d just handed her the most important clue in the world. The way she looked at him made his chest feel tight, a pleasant, aching pressure. It was better than any praise he’d ever received in the navy. It was real.

"It changes everything," she agreed, her voice firm. "It means whoever did it had access to the office, knew the key was there, and knew exactly when Tarrant would be inspecting the race. It wasn't a slip. It was an ambush."

"And Vane," Kit said, the name tasting sour in his mouth, "was the one who told you the key was lost. The one who took all the ledgers 'for safekeeping'. The one who was polishing that lantern just last week."

Nell’s jaw tightened. She looked from the lantern back to him, a silent question passing between them. They were partners in this now, more than ever. He saw her resolve harden, and it mirrored his own.

"Thank you, Kit," she said, her voice quiet but strong. "Thank you for bringing this to me."

"It’s the truth," he said simply. He didn't need anything else.

* * *

Nell spent the rest of the afternoon in a state of heightened alert, the brass lantern a cold, heavy weight on her desk. She called Mr. Pell from his back room, and together they examined the stamp again, confirming its origin. Vane’s story was unravelling, thread by thread. But she needed more. She needed to know what Silas Rudd knew, if anything.

As dusk began to settle, painting the sky in shades of bruised purple and orange, Nell decided to walk past the Plough Inn. Silas was staying there, a fact Aunt Maud had gleefully reported over a rather cold cup of tea. Nell hoped for a word, a chance to gauge his reaction to his brother-in-law’s death.

The inn was busy, its low roar of conversation and laughter spilling out into the yard. Nell kept to the shadows of the wall, her eyes scanning the dim corners by the stables. And there he was. Silas Rudd, leaning against a pile of sacks, a half-empty bottle in his hand. He wasn't alone. Another man, one of the rougher types from the docks, stood with him, their heads close together as they spoke in low, urgent tones.

As Nell watched, Silas pulled a thick sheaf of papers from his inside pocket and thrust them into the other man's hands. The man nodded, then produced a small oil lamp from his own coat. He struck a match, and the flame illuminated the papers for a moment before Silas grabbed them back and tossed them onto a small, iron brazier that was already glowing with embers.

Nell’s heart lurched. Papers. Burning. She took a step closer, her breath catching in her throat.

"What are you doing?" she called out, her voice sharper than she intended.

Silas Rudd whirled around, his face a mask of shock and fury, quickly replaced by a belligerent scowl. "Get lost, Ashby. This is none of your affair."

"I think it might be," Nell said, taking another step into the yard. "Those look like business papers. Financial papers."

"Bugger your financial papers," Silas snarled, his eyes wild. He grabbed a long metal poker from beside the brazier and jabbed it at the papers, sending a shower of sparks and ash into the air. "This is private business. Now piss off before I decide you're a problem."

He took a threatening step towards her, the smell of drink and sweat rolling off him in waves. Nell held her ground, but a prickle of fear ran down her spine. This was not the grieving, weak brother-in-law Ivy had described. This was a man with something to hide, and a temper to match.

"I'm not leaving, Silas," Nell said, her voice steady despite her racing heart. "Not until you tell me what you're burning."

He laughed, a harsh, grating sound. "Or what? You'll run to your precious little solicitor? Or maybe to that lock-keeper of yours? I've heard the town talk. You two are thick as thieves."

He raised the poker again, and for a moment Nell thought he might use it. Then, with a final, vicious shove, he sent the remaining papers tumbling into the heart of the brazier. The flames leapt up, devouring the paper in a greedy orange rush. Silas turned his back on her and stalked back towards the inn's rear entrance, disappearing inside without another word.

Nell stood frozen for a moment, watching the brazier. The papers were gone, reduced to curling black ash and smoke. She had seen nothing conclusive, no names, no figures. Just the frantic, guilty act of a man destroying evidence. Whether it was evidence of Owen Tarrant's murder, or evidence of Silas's own gambling debts, she couldn't be sure. But it was a clue, nonetheless. A red herring, perhaps, but one that had burned itself into her memory.

She turned away from the heat of the brazier and walked back into the cold, darkening street, the image of Silas's furious face seared onto her mind. The lantern on her desk was the key, she was sure of it. But Silas Rudd’s fire was a warning. She was getting closer, and someone was beginning to notice.
