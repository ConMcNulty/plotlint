# Chapter 8: The_Fete

The kitchen was thick with the sweet, buttery scent of baking. Aunt Maud stood at the table, her sleeves rolled back and dusted with flour, her brow furrowed in concentration as she beat a mixture for a Victoria sponge. Nell, with a practiced hand, rolled out scone dough on the marble slab, the rhythmic thud of the rolling pin a steady counterpoint to the clatter of crockery. Outside, the September sun struggled through a high, thin layer of cloud, promising a day of deceptive brightness.

"Well, that's the scones in," Aunt Maud declared, wiping her hands on her apron. "Now, we just need to get this cake in the oven before it curdles. You'll have to keep an eye on it, Nell. I must go and see about the jam stall. Mrs. Tibbs has been in a state all week about it."

Nell nodded, her mind elsewhere. "I'll be along shortly, Aunt Maud. Just let me finish these."

As she cut the scones into perfect rounds and slid them onto a baking tray, Aunt Maud bustled about, collecting the empty bowls and flour sacks. She paused by the stove, watching her niece with a quiet, knowing gaze.

"You're thinking about it again, aren't you?" she said, her voice gentle.

Nell sighed. "I'm sorry, Aunt. It's hard not to. Everyone's talking about it."

"I should think so," Aunt Maud agreed, her gaze drifting towards the window. "A man found drowned in his own mill race. It's not the sort of thing happens every day in Lessing Fen. The coroner said accident, but you don't believe that, do you?"

"No," Nell said, her voice firm. "I don't. And you know why. The sluice gate was opened with a key. Mr. Tarrant didn't just slip; someone pushed him in."

Aunt Maud tutted. "Such a dreadful thought. And who would have a reason to do such a thing? Mr. Tarrant was a good man, ran a fair mill."

"He was also a man who kept his own counsel," Nell countered, thinking back to the sparse, impersonal house she had inventoried. "And he was about to have the books audited by the bank, according to Mr. Hollis. The week he died."

"Ah, the books," Aunt Maud murmured, her eyes narrowing. "That bookkeeper of his, Mr. Vane. He was ever so helpful when you went to the house, wasn't he? Hovering about, offering you tea and the keys."

"He was," Nell agreed. "And he took the ledgers away for safekeeping, he said. But the ledger for the last quarter was missing from the shelf. He claimed Mr. Tarrant had taken it to the mill."

"Convenient," Aunt Maud said, a note of suspicion in her voice. "And that clay on his boots. That poor lock-keeper, Mr. Merrow. The whole town is pointing its finger at him, just because he holds one of the keys. It's not fair. He seems a quiet sort. Did he say anything else to you?"

Nell remembered the walk along the towpath, the awkward gratitude in Kit Merrow's eyes, the shared understanding that had passed between them. "He said he saw a lantern moving along the sluice bank that night. He thought it was Mr. Tarrant. But the lantern we found… it was stamped 'Tarrant Mill Office'. It came from the mill, not from the house."

Aunt Maud considered this. "So the person who opened the gate took the lantern from the office with them. And the key from the hook in the office."

"Yes," Nell said, her brow furrowed. "But Mr. Vane said the key had been lost for months. He said Mr. Tarrant kept meaning to get another one made."

"Hmph," Aunt Maud grunted. "A bit too convenient, that. And what about the widow's brother? That Mr. Rudd. I heard he owed his brother-in-law a pretty penny. Gambler, they say."

"He does," Nell confirmed. "And I saw him burning papers behind the Plough Inn yesterday. It was very suspicious."

"Was it anything to do with the murder, though? Or just his own debts? A man with debts to hide is a man with secrets, but it doesn't make him a murderer." Aunt Maud sighed, picking up a lemon and beginning to zest it. "It's all such a muddle. The open gate, the missing ledger, the clay, the empty hook, the burning papers… it feels like pieces of a puzzle that don't quite fit together."

Nell placed the scones in the oven and closed the door with a heavy click. "That's the trouble, Aunt. They don't fit. Not yet."

* * *

The church fete was in full swing on the green. A bunting of faded red, white, and blue fluttered from the branches of the old oak tree, and the air was filled with the noise of children's laughter, the shouts of the tug-of-war team, and the tinny music from a hurdy-gurdy. Nell walked slowly through the throng, a small wicker basket in her hand, her eyes scanning the familiar faces of the townspeople. She was here to help, but her mind was elsewhere, turning over the same facts again and again.

She stopped at the jam stall, a long table laden with pots of ruby-red, golden-yellow, and deep purple preserves. Mrs. Tibbs, a woman whose face was as round and red as one of her tomatoes, beamed at her.

"Nell my dear! Just the person. I was hoping you'd come and judge the jam competition. We have a new entry this year, from young Mr. Merrow over at the lock. He's made a damson. Said he used the fruit from the bushes down by the river."

Nell was surprised. "Mr. Merrow? He made jam?"

"He did," Mrs. Tibbs said proudly, holding up a jar. "Look at the colour. A proper deep purple. It's a handsome thing, isn't it?"

Nell took the jar. It was indeed handsome, the glass clear, the label neatly written in a firm, precise hand. 'Damson Jam. K.M. 1923'. She thought of Kit, his quiet hands on the lock mechanism, his eyes fixed on the water, and found it hard to imagine him standing over a bubbling pot of fruit. But there it was, the evidence. It was a small, normal piece of life, a world away from murder.

"I'll be happy to judge it, Mrs. Tibbs," she said. "It looks wonderful."

She bought the jar and moved on, her gaze drifting across the green. She saw Constable Dorrit leaning against the maypole, talking to the blacksmith, a half-eaten sandwich in his hand. She saw Ivy Tarrant, a fragile figure in black, standing alone by the tombstones in the churchyard, her gaze fixed on the distant mill. She saw Ambrose Vane circulating, his genial face a picture of sorrow, offering tea to the vicar's wife and pointing out the prize-winning marrow. He looked like a man who belonged, a pillar of the community, not a man with a secret that could send him to the gallows.

Nell watched him for a moment, then turned her attention to the tug-of-war. The mill workers were losing badly to the team from the drainage board. The mill workers, she noted, were strong and burly, but the drainage board men were lean and wiry, their movements practised and economical. Ambrose Vane was not among them. He was on the sidelines, applauding with the rest of the spectators. A genial, helpful man.

She finished her circuit of the stalls, ate a scone with strawberry jam, and watched the vicar give a short speech about the harvest. She saw nobody connected with the case who said or did anything out of the ordinary. The day was warm, the fete was a success, and the mystery of Owen Tarrant's death remained exactly where it had been that morning: a collection of cold, hard facts that refused to form a coherent picture.

* * *

That evening, Nell sat at her desk in the front parlour, the oil lamp casting a warm pool of light on the wood. The rest of the house was quiet; Aunt Maud had gone to bed early, worn out by the excitement of the day. On the desk lay her notebook, its pages filled with her careful, looping script. She picked up her fountain pen and uncapped it, the scent of ink sharp in the still air.

She began to copy out her notes, writing in a fair, steady hand, as if the act of transcribing the facts might somehow clarify them. She started at the beginning.

*Owen Tarrant found drowned in the mill race. Sluice gate found open. Coroner rules accident.*

She wrote beneath it what she knew for certain.

*Only three keys fit the sluice gate: Kit Merrow's (lock-keeper), Owen Tarrant's (mill owner), and the one kept on a hook in the mill office.*

She paused, dipping the pen in the inkwell. The next point was more of a question.

*The key on the hook in the mill office was empty the morning after the death. Ambrose Vane claims it has been lost for months. Owen Tarrant was planning to have another cut.*

She moved on to the ledger.

*The last quarter's ledger is missing from the house. Ambrose Vane took the other ledgers 'for safekeeping'. He said Owen must have taken the missing one to the mill.*

Her pen scratched across the page. And then there was the physical evidence.

*Ambrose Vane's boots had dried grey fen clay caked in the wells after the inquest. The clay of the sluice bank. He told the coroner he was at home in town all night.*

And the lantern.

*A storm lantern, stamped 'Tarrant Mill Office', was found hidden in the reeds below the sluice. It had been used on the night of the murder.*

Finally, the last piece of the puzzle she had witnessed herself.

*Silas Rudd, Ivy Tarrant's brother, was seen burning papers behind the Plough Inn. He was agitated and refused to speak when confronted.*

She read through what she had written. It was all there, laid out as plain as day. The open gate. The missing ledger. The clay. The empty hook. The lantern. The fire. The facts were unchanged. They were the same facts she had started with that morning, the same facts she had carried with her all day through the noisy, cheerful chaos of the fete. They were facts that pointed in different directions, facts that contradicted each other, facts that formed a wall around the truth.

She closed the notebook with a soft snap. The oil lamp hissed softly. Outside, the town was settling down for the night. Nell felt no closer to an answer than she had when she had first walked to Mr. Pell's office and found the crowd at the mill race. The sluice gate was still open. The key was still missing. The murderer was still out there. She was no further on, and no different. She blew out the lamp and went to bed, the same questions echoing in the dark.
