# Chapter 9: Debts_and_Wills

The next morning, the air was sharp and clear, promising a day of frost before the sun had properly risen. Nell, her breath pluming in the cold, walked with purpose towards the house where Silas Rudd lodged with his sister. The events of the previous evening, the silent frustration of her notes, had crystallised a resolve. The burning papers were a loose end, and loose ends, she had learned, had a way of tripping a person up. Silas Rudd might be a red herring, but she needed to see the colour for herself.

Ivy Tarrant opened the door, her face a pale mask of grief. She looked Nell up and down, her eyes red-rimmed but dry. "Miss Ashby. To what do I owe this… intrusion?"

"It's about my work for the estate, Mrs. Tarrant. And about your brother. I was hoping to have a word with him."

Ivy’s brittle composure seemed to fray at the edges. "Silas? Why? He has nothing to do with any of it. He's just a fool."

"He was seen burning papers behind the Plough Inn last night," Nell said, keeping her voice even. "I thought it best to understand why."

A flicker of something—shame, perhaps, or irritation—crossed Ivy’s face. "You'll find him in the back parlour. He's been up all night, the idiot." She gestured vaguely down the hall. "But don't say I didn't warn you."

The back parlour was small and cluttered, smelling of stale tobacco and damp wool. Silas Rudd sat slumped in a worn armchair, his face in his hands. He looked up as Nell entered, his eyes puffy and bloodshot. For a moment, he looked like a trapped animal, then his shoulders slumped in a gesture of weary defeat.

"Come to gawk?" he grumbled, his voice rough.

"No, Mr. Rudd. I came to understand," Nell replied, taking a seat opposite him. "You were burning papers last night."

He let out a short, harsh laugh. "What of it? It's my own business."

"I saw you," Nell pressed. "You were agitated. I think it has to do with the debt you owed my late employer, Mr. Tarrant."

Silas stared at the floor for a long moment, then looked up, his expression defiant but his eyes pleading. "The old man," he said, his voice thick. "He was a hard man, Miss Ashby. A hard, hard man. He lent me money when I was at the end of my tether, but he made me pay it back, every penny, with interest. It was a millstone around my neck."

"So you had a motive to argue with him," Nell said, though she felt a cold certainty that this was a dead end.

"A motive? I had a motive to hate him, yes. But not to kill him. He forgave me." Silas pushed himself out of the chair and went to a small desk in the corner. He rummaged in a drawer and pulled out a single sheet of paper, which he held out to Nell. "Here. See for yourself."

Nell took the letter. It was written in Owen Tarrant’s firm, clear hand. 'To Silas Rudd, I hereby forgive the sum of two hundred and fifty pounds, being the full amount of all monies lent to you by me. This debt is cancelled. Owen Tarrant.' It was dated nine days ago.

"I found it on his desk yesterday," Silas said, his voice trembling slightly. "After he was… after they found him. I went to his house, to see if Ivy was alright, and I saw it there. I owed him that money for years, and he just… wrote it off. And I'd been shouting at him the week before, calling him a usurer, a tyrant. Made a proper fool of myself. When I found that letter… I felt like such a fool. And a coward. So I burned the IOUs. I couldn't bear to look at them anymore."

He sank back into his chair, the fight gone out of him. "There. Now you know. I'm a fool and a coward, but I'm not a murderer. The debt's gone. I have no reason to hurt him."

Nell studied the letter, then looked at the man slumped before her. The raw shame on his face was more convincing than any alibi. The red herring, she thought, was finally accounted for. Silas Rudd was not the killer. The debt was cancelled. The motive was gone. She folded the letter and handed it back to him.

"Thank you, Mr. Rudd," she said, standing. "I'm sorry to have troubled you."

He didn't answer, just stared at the floor, a man relieved of a burden he hadn't even realised he was still carrying.

* * *

The walk back to Mr. Pell’s office felt different. The crisp morning air seemed to carry a new weight, a new purpose. Silas Rudd was innocent. The motive she had been chasing, however flimsy, was gone. But that left the other facts, the ones that felt more solid, more dangerous. The missing ledger. The empty hook. The new will. She needed to see that will.

Mr. Pell was dozing in his chair when she entered, a shawl draped over his knees. He awoke with a start, blinking at her. "Ah, Miss Ashby. Back from your… walk?"

"Yes, sir. I’ve finished with the Rudd papers for now. I was hoping to look at Mr. Tarrant’s will. The final one."

Pell rubbed his eyes. "Of course, of course. It's in the strongbox. Key's in the top drawer of my desk." He fumbled for the key and handed it to her. "Be careful with it, will you? It's all very final, these documents."

The strongbox was an old, heavy thing, bound with iron. Nell turned the key in the lock and lifted the heavy lid. Inside, nestled amongst stacks of deeds and bond certificates, was a single envelope, sealed with wax. She broke the seal and slid out the document. It was a codicil to Tarrant’s will, dated nine days before his death. Her heart began to beat a little faster as she read.

'I, Owen Tarrant, being of sound mind and body, do hereby revoke all previous bequests to my junior partner, Ambrose Vane. Upon my death, the partnership between myself and Ambrose Vane is hereby dissolved, and he shall receive no further share in the mill or its profits. Furthermore, all books and documents pertaining to the Fen Drainage Board fund, currently held in trust by the mill, are to be surrendered directly to the manager of the Fen & County Bank for immediate audit. I appoint no executor for this matter. Witnessed this ninth day of October, 1923.'

Nell read it twice. The cold, hard logic of it was chilling. Tarrant had discovered the theft, confronted Vane, and in this new will, he had cut him out completely and ensured the theft would be exposed. The motive was no longer a possibility; it was a certainty. Ambrose Vane stood to lose everything—his position, his livelihood, and quite possibly his freedom. The genial bookkeeper, the helpful partner, the man who offered tea and keys, now seemed to Nell like a man with a noose tightening around his neck.

She carefully refolded the will and replaced it in the envelope, her mind racing. Vane knew she was going through the papers. He had hovered over her at the house, he had asked about the inventory. He knew she could find this. And he had seen her notice the clay on his boots. He knew she was asking questions.

* * *

The mill office was quiet when Nell arrived that afternoon. Ambrose Vane was behind his desk, meticulously balancing a column of figures in a ledger, his back to the door. He turned as she entered, and his face lit up with that familiar, warm smile.

"Miss Ashby! To what do I owe the pleasure? Come to find any more little mysteries in the papers?"

"Not at the moment, Mr. Vane," she said, her own voice carefully neutral. "I just thought I'd finish up the last of the loose ends in here."

"Of course, of course," he said, rising and gesturing to the chair opposite his desk. "Please, sit. Can I offer you some tea? I've just put the kettle on. A nice cup of tea helps the head, when one is dealing with so many figures."

"No, thank you," Nell declined. "I won't keep you long."

"Nonsense," he said, already moving towards the small stove in the corner. "It's no trouble. One must take care of oneself." He bustled about, filling a kettle, finding a clean cup. The easy, helpful manner was as ingrained as his neat parting. He placed a steaming cup of tea before her and sat back down, folding his hands on the desk. He watched her, his eyes sharp and assessing.

"It's a terrible business, isn't it?" he said, his voice low and confidential. "Poor Mr. Tarrant. One slip in the dark, and… well. The coroner was quite right, of course. These things happen."

"Yes, I suppose they do," Nell said, taking a sip of the tea. It was hot and strong. "Though it must be a terrible shock for Mrs. Tarrant."

"She's holding up," Vane said, his expression one of genuine sympathy. "A strong woman, Ivy. It's a comfort to see her bearing up so well. Speaking of which, did you manage to sort out that little business with her brother? The gambling debts? I do hope he wasn't causing any trouble."

"No, it's all sorted," Nell said, a little surprised by his interest. "Mr. Tarrant had forgiven the debt."

"Ah, how very generous of him," Vane murmured. He leaned forward slightly, his gaze fixed on her. "And the will? Have you found everything you need for the probate? I do hope it's all straightforward. I just want to see everything settled properly for Mrs. Tarrant's sake. The mill is a complicated concern, as you know."

"The will is being handled by Mr. Pell," Nell said, her guard rising. "He's an expert in these matters."

"Of course, of course," Vane said, raising his hands in a gesture of placid agreement. "I'm sure he is. It's just that I was Mr. Tarrant's partner for so many years. One feels a certain… responsibility. To ensure everything is above board." He picked up his own cup and took a slow, deliberate sip. The office was silent, save for the ticking of the tall clock in the corner. The cheerful, helpful demeanour had not changed, but Nell felt the atmosphere in the room shift, become denser, more charged.

He set his cup down and looked at her, his smile still in place but his eyes holding a new intensity. "You know, Miss Ashby, this is a fenland town. The river can be treacherous, especially at night. And the sluice bank… well, it's not a place for a young woman to be wandering about after dark. The ground is soft, the paths are treacherous. One could easily slip, just as poor Mr. Tarrant did. A terrible accident."

He paused, letting the words hang in the air. The pleasant, helpful tone was still there, but beneath it was a distinct warning. Nell felt a prickle of unease run down her spine. She looked at the empty hook by the door, the place where the sluice key should have been. She thought of the clay on his boots, of the missing ledger, of the will she had read that morning. The genial mask was still in place, but she was no longer fooled by what it hid.

"I'm sure you're right, Mr. Vane," she said, her voice steady. "It is dangerous." She stood up. "Thank you for the tea. I'll be going now."

"Of course, of course," he said, rising with her. He walked her to the door, his smile unwavering. "Do take care of yourself, Miss Ashby. We wouldn't want any more… accidents."

Nell stepped out of the mill office and into the afternoon light, the slam of the door behind her sounding strangely final. She stood for a moment on the cobbles, her heart thumping a steady, heavy rhythm against her ribs. The warning had been clear. Ambrose Vane knew she was getting too close. And he was not a man who would take kindly to being cornered. The sluice gate was still open. The key was still missing. And the murderer, she now knew without a doubt, was watching her.
