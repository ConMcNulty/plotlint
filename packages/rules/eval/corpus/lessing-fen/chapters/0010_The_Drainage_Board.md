# Chapter 10: The_Drainage_Board

The vestry of St Cedd's church smelled of damp stone, beeswax, and the faint, sweet tang of last Sunday's flowers. It was a cool, quiet place, a world away from the close atmosphere of Ambrose Vane's rooms. Nell sat at the long, scarred table, a stack of heavy, leather-bound volumes before her. Kit Merrow sat opposite, his brow furrowed as he turned the pages of a minute book from the Fen Drainage Board, his large, calloused hands surprisingly gentle with the old paper.

'There it is,' he said, tapping a finger on a line. 'October, last year. "Payment received from Tarrant's Mill for drainage repairs to the North Bank, sum of two hundred and fifty pounds."'

Nell nodded, her own book open to the corresponding entry in the mill's ledger, which they had managed to procure from Mr. Hollis at the bank under the pretence of the estate inventory. She found the same figure, written in a neat, looping script she now recognised as Ambrose Vane's.

'And now,' Kit said, turning to the Board's own account book, 'we see what the Board actually received. Two hundred and fifty pounds.' He looked at her, his expression blank. 'That's the same. So where's the problem?'

'The problem,' Nell said, pointing to the next entry in the mill's ledger, 'is here. Three months later. "Payment transferred to Fen Drainage Board for upkeep of the East Ditch, sum of three hundred pounds." She looked up at him. 'Let's see what the Board has for that.'

Kit turned the page. He scanned the columns, his lips moving silently. Then he stopped. He looked back at Nell, his eyes wide. 'There's no entry for three hundred pounds from Tarrant's Mill. There's an entry for two hundred.'

Nell leaned forward, her own heart starting to beat a little faster. 'Show me.'

He pushed the book across the table. She ran her finger down the neat columns of figures. There it was, plain as day. A payment of two hundred pounds, received from Tarrant's Mill, dated two days after the mill's ledger claimed to have sent three hundred. The missing hundred pounds was a ghost, a sum that had been written out of the mill's books as if it had never existed.

'There are others,' Nell whispered, flipping back through the Board's book. 'Look. April. "Payment for sluice gate maintenance, four hundred pounds." And in the mill's books, it's four hundred.' She found the next one. 'June. "Payment for weed clearance, one hundred and fifty pounds." And in the mill's books, it's one hundred and fifty.' She looked up at Kit, her face pale. 'It's all in Vane's hand. He's been skimming it, bit by bit. For years.'

The implications settled over them like a cold fog. It wasn't a single, desperate act. It was a steady, careful draining of a public fund, a fund that Tarrant's Mill was supposed to be safeguarding. And Owen Tarrant, by demanding an audit, had put a stop to it. He had found the hole in the accounts and was plugging it. And that, Nell thought, was why he was dead. The motive was no longer a whisper in a will; it was a cold, hard, documented fact.

* * *

The walk back to the town was a silent one. The late afternoon sun cast long shadows across the fen, and the only sounds were the rustle of reeds and the distant cry of a curlew. Nell's mind was a whirlwind of figures and faces, of Ambrose Vane's genial smile and the damning proof in their hands. Kit walked beside her, his hands shoved deep into his pockets, his gaze fixed on the muddy path ahead.

'The county police,' he said, his voice rough. 'That's what we do. We take this to them. To Inspector Croft in Ely. He'll listen. He's not Dorrit.'

Nell stopped walking. She turned to face him, her own voice tight with frustration. 'And what will he do, Kit? Send a constable down? They'll look at this, they'll talk to Vane, and Vane will talk circles around them. He's a respected man. He'll offer them tea, he'll be devastated by the suggestion, he'll have a plausible explanation for everything, and they'll go away and nothing will change. It will be just like the inquest.'

'This isn't the inquest,' Kit insisted, his jaw clenched. 'This is proof. Real proof. Not just mud on boots or a missing key. This is paper. It's solid.'

'Is it?' Nell shot back. 'While we're waiting for the county to get off their backsides, what happens? Vane knows we have this. He saw the look on my face when I left his rooms. He knows I'm not just some clerk doing an inventory. He knows I'm asking questions. He warned me.' She shuddered, remembering the pleasant, chilling tone of his voice. 'And now we have proof that he's a thief. What do you think a man like that will do when he's cornered? Wait for the police? Or get rid of the problem? The problem, Kit, is us.'

He stared at her, his face unreadable. 'You can't live like that. Jumping at shadows.'

'I am not jumping at shadows,' she said, her voice rising. 'I am looking at a man who has stolen thousands of pounds from a public fund and killed his partner to cover it up. I am looking at a man who threatened me this afternoon. I am not being fanciful, I am being careful.'

'And I'm telling you this is too big for you to handle on your own,' he said, his voice low and urgent. 'You're not a copper, Nell. You're a clerk. Let the professionals deal with it. For God's sake, just be safe.'

'The professionals,' she said, her voice dripping with sarcasm, 'are Constable Dorrit and a cup of tea. No. I will not do nothing. I will not wait for him to make me the next accident.'

'Is that what this is about?' he asked, his eyes flashing. 'Proving you're right? Getting some kind of credit from it? Because this is dangerous. It's not a game.'

'It's not a game,' she said, her voice trembling with anger and fear. 'It's a murder. And it's my duty to see justice is done, not yours or the county's or Dorrit's. It's mine.' She turned away from him, her back stiff. 'I suggest you go back to your lock. It's safer there.'

For a long moment, neither of them moved. Then she heard him let out a harsh breath, a sound of pure frustration. He didn't touch her, didn't say another word. He just turned and strode away, back towards the river and the lock, his shoulders set in a rigid line. Nell stood alone on the path, the weight of the knowledge in her hands suddenly feeling like a stone around her neck. The argument had torn something between them, and she was left with only the cold certainty of what she had to do, and the chilling fear of what might come of it.

* * *

The house was quiet when Nell let herself in, the kind of quiet that felt heavy and expectant. Aunt Maud was out, visiting a neighbour. The familiar comfort of her own sitting-room was gone, replaced by a sense of unease. She went straight to her bedroom, her steps hesitant. The window was slightly ajar. She had left it firmly shut that morning.

A cold dread seeped into her bones. She pushed the window up the rest of the way, the rusty screech loud in the silent room. She peered out. The small, neat garden below was undisturbed. The latch on the window was old and stiff; it could have slipped, she told herself, but she knew she had secured it. She turned from the window and her eyes went straight to her desk, where she had left her case notes, the neat sheets of paper detailing every clue, every suspicion.

They were gone.

The desk was clear, except for a faint outline in the dust where the papers had been. Someone had been in her room. Someone had taken them. Her heart hammered against her ribs, a frantic, panicked beat. She looked around the room, her gaze darting from the wardrobe to the chest of drawers, as if expecting to see a figure lurking in the shadows. There was no one. Only the silence, pressing in on her.

She sank into the chair by the desk, her legs suddenly weak. She didn't dare turn on the lamp, afraid that the sudden burst of light would announce her presence to whoever might be outside, watching. She sat in the gathering dusk, the room growing darker around her, and for the first time since she had begun her inventory, she felt truly, utterly alone. The murderer wasn't just a face in a crowd or a name in a ledger anymore. He had been here. In her room. He knew what she knew. And he was not a man who would hesitate to silence her for good.
