# Chapter 11: Bait

The first light of morning found Nell Ashby at her desk, her face pale but her eyes clear. The fear of the night before had curdled into a cold, hard resolve. She could not go to the police; Constable Dorrit would laugh her out of the police house. She could not go to Mr. Pell; he would be horrified and insist she drop the matter, for the sake of the town's peace. She could not go to the county; they would see a market town clerk with wild suspicions and no proof. She was alone. And the man who had taken her notes knew it.

The only thing she had left was the knowledge that he wanted what he had taken. He believed he was safe, that his secrets were his own again. He believed the missing ledger was gone for good. Nell would use that belief. She would set a trap.

She dressed in her best grey coat and hat, the one she wore for calling on clients, and walked to the mill office. The air was crisp, and the river mist lay low over the fields, clinging to the reeds like a shroud. She did not look toward the sluice gate. She did not need to see it.

Ambrose Vane was in the office, as neat and precise as ever. He was sitting at his desk, a cup of tea steaming before him, and he looked up with a smile that was warm but did not quite reach his eyes.

"Miss Ashby," he said, rising. "A pleasure. Can I offer you a cup of tea? It's a dreadful morning."

"No, thank you, Mr. Vane," Nell said, her voice steady. "I'm only here on a small matter of the estate. I was just tidying up some loose ends."

"Oh?" Vane prompted, gesturing for her to sit. "How is the inventory progressing?"

"Very well, thank you. In fact, I have some good news. I found it."

Vane's brow furrowed slightly. "Found what, my dear?"

"The last-quarter ledger," Nell said, keeping her tone as casual as if she were discussing the weather. "The one that went missing. It was tucked away in a box of old correspondence at the house. Quite a relief, as I believe Mr. Hollis at the bank was rather keen to see it."

A flicker of something—surprise, perhaps, or calculation—crossed Vane's face. It was gone so quickly Nell might have imagined it, but she had not. He recovered his geniality with a practiced ease.

"Excellent! That is splendid news. I was dreadfully worried about that. Owen was so terribly forgetful at times."

"He was," Nell agreed. "So I've told Mr. Hollis he can collect it from here tomorrow morning. I thought it best to keep it in the safe here at the mill, rather than move it again."

"Of course, of course," Vane said, nodding vigorously. "That's the sensible place. I'll be sure to be here to let him in. The safe key is on its hook, as always." He gestured to the board by the door, where a single key hung. Nell's gaze flickered to it, noting the empty space where a third key should have been. "No trouble at all. A great weight off my mind, I must say. That Drainage Board business is so very particular."

"Yes," Nell said, standing up. "So I understand." She gave him a small, formal nod. "Thank you, Mr. Vane. I'll leave you to your work."

She walked back to Wick Street with her head held high, but her heart was a drum against her ribs. It was a gamble, a desperate one, but it was the only move she had left.

* * *

Dusk was settling by the time Nell reached the little lock-keeper's cottage, its single warm light a beacon in the gathering gloom. Kit Merrow was on the towpath, his back to the river, staring out over the water. He turned as she approached, and the worry in his eyes was plain to see.

"Nell," he said, his voice rough. He took off his cap, twisting it in his hands. "About this morning. I was a fool. A damned fool. I can't... I can't stand by and watch you face this alone. Not when it's my fault you're in it."

"It's not your fault, Kit," she said, her voice softening.

"It is," he insisted, his gaze intense. "If I'd gone to the county police myself, like I should have—"

"But you didn't, because you knew Dorrit would make it worse, and you were protecting me," Nell finished for him. "And I'm grateful. Truly. But I've made my choice. I have to see this through." She took a step closer. "But I'm glad you're here now."

He looked at her, really looked at her, and she saw the fear and the admiration warring in his expression. "What have you done?" he asked, his voice low.

"I've baited the trap," she said quietly. "I told Vane the missing ledger has turned up and that Hollis is collecting it from the mill office safe tomorrow morning. He'll believe it's gone for good, and he'll want to make sure it's gone for good."

Kit's eyes widened with understanding. "He'll go tonight. To the safe."

"He'll go tonight," Nell confirmed. "To make sure it's still there, or to take it himself, or both."

"Alone?" Kit asked.

"Alone," Nell said. "He'll think he's unobserved."

"Then I'm coming with you," Kit stated, his voice hardening with resolve. "There's no two ways about it."

"No, Kit," Nell said, reaching out and placing a hand on his arm. "He's a strong man, and he knows the mill. If he sees us, it could be dangerous. I need you to be my eyes and ears from a distance. Your knowledge of the river and the bank is the best weapon I have."

He looked from her determined face to the dark, silent river, then back to her. He gave a slow, reluctant nod. "Alright. But if anything feels wrong, anything at all, you get out. You shout. You run. I'll be there."

"I know," she whispered.

He reached into his pocket and pulled out a small, heavy brass key. It was old and worn, its teeth smooth from use. He took her hand and pressed it into her palm.

"My key," he said, his voice thick. "The sluice key. You hold on to it. As a promise. That you'll be careful. And as a sign of trust."

She closed her fingers around the cool metal, a solid weight in her hand. She looked up at him, and the world seemed to narrow to the space between them, the fading light, the sound of the water. He leaned down, and she met him halfway. It was not the awkward, fumbling kiss of the bridge. It was a kiss of shared purpose and fear and a growing, undeniable affection. It was a promise.

* * *

The mill office was silent and still when Nell and Kit returned just before midnight. The moon was a sliver, casting long, distorted shadows across the green. Kit led her to a small, low door set high in the gable wall of the building. It was a loft, used for storing old sacks and bales of cloth.

"Here," he whispered, helping her up the rickety ladder. "This is the best vantage point. You can see the door of the office, and the path along the sluice bank."

Nell scrambled into the cramped, dusty space, the air thick with the smell of dry rot and grain. Kit followed, pulling the ladder up after him. They knelt in the darkness, pressed shoulder to shoulder, their eyes fixed on the sliver of the world visible through the grimy window.

Time stretched. The only sounds were the rustle of the reeds in the faint breeze and the distant hoot of an owl. Nell’s legs began to cramp, and her nerves were frayed raw. Was he coming? Had he seen through her ruse? Had he simply given up?

Then, a pinprick of light appeared on the far end of the sluice bank. It bobbed and swayed, growing steadily larger. A lantern. Nell’s breath caught in her throat. Kit’s hand found hers in the darkness, his grip firm and reassuring.

The light stopped at the mill door. A figure stood there for a moment, silhouetted against the weak moonlight. Then, a key turned in the lock. The door opened, the figure slipped inside, and the door closed, plunging the path back into darkness.

"It's him," Nell breathed.

They waited, hearts pounding. Moments later, the office door opened again, and the figure emerged, carrying the lantern. He walked along the bank, his pace unhurried, and disappeared around the corner toward the lock.

"Now," Kit whispered, and they scrambled back down the ladder.

They crept to the office door. It was unlocked. Kit pushed it open, and they slipped inside, closing it quietly behind them. The air in the office was still, smelling of paper, ink, and Vane's expensive tea. Kit went to the key board by the door and retrieved his own key, replacing it on its nail. He then led Nell to the safe, a heavy iron box bolted to the floor.

"He won't have had time to open it," Nell whispered, her hand hovering over the dial. "He just wanted to see if it was still there."

"Or to make sure the key was on the hook," Kit countered. He pointed to the safe door. "Look."

Nell leaned closer. There was a faint scratch on the metal, just below the keyhole. It was fresh. Kit tried the key from the hook on the board. It turned smoothly in the lock. He swung the heavy door open. Inside, nestled in a felt-lined tray, lay the last-quarter ledger.

"He was here," Nell said, her voice a triumphant whisper. "He came in, and he checked the safe."

"And the key," Kit added, his eyes fixed on the scratch. "He tested the key on the safe. To make sure it still worked. He was ready for anything."

Nell reached out and took the ledger from the safe. It was heavy, solid proof of Vane's guilt. She closed the safe door, and Kit locked it, returning the key to its hook. They stood in the dimness of the office, the lantern light from outside casting long shadows across the walls. Nell clutched the ledger to her chest, the cold hard cover a shield against the danger that lurked outside.

They had him. They had the proof. The trap had sprung. Now all they had to do was survive the night.
