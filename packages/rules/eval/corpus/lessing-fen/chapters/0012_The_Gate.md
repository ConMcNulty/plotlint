# Chapter 12: The_Gate

Nell held the ledger, its weight a solid, undeniable thing in her hands. The air in the office was thick with the scent of damp paper and dust, and the faint, sweet tang of Vane's tea from earlier in the day. Outside, the lantern light on the walkway was still, a single yellow eye fixed on the office window. Kit stood beside her, his own body a tense line, his hand resting on the back of a wooden chair, ready to use it as a weapon if needed.

"He's waiting," Kit murmured, his voice low. "He knows we're in here."

Nell knew it. She could feel it, a prickling tension on the back of her neck. The silence outside was too complete. Vane wasn't going to run. He was waiting for them to make their move. She looked at the ledger, then at the safe. The scratch on the door, the key on the hook. It was all there. The trap was sprung, but the trapper was still at large, and he knew they had him.

She took a step towards the door. "He won't come in here," she said, her voice firmer than she felt. "Not with the lock on our side."

Kit nodded, his eyes never leaving the window. "He'll want to talk first. Confuse us. Make us doubt."

"He won't succeed," Nell said, though her heart was thumping a frantic rhythm against her ribs. She walked to the door, Kit a shadow behind her. She took a deep breath and eased the door open just enough to see out.

Ambrose Vane was standing on the walkway, not two paces away. He wasn't holding a lantern, but the moonlight was enough to illuminate his face. The genial mask was gone, replaced by a cold, hard emptiness. He didn't look surprised to see them. He looked resigned.

"Nell," he said, his voice smooth as ever, but with a new, chilling edge. "And Kit. I was hoping you'd be here."

Nell pushed the door wider and stepped out onto the walkway, the ledger held tight to her chest. Kit followed, positioning himself slightly behind her. The wood was damp and slick under her shoes. The sluice race roared below them, a constant, hungry sound.

"Ambrose," Nell said. "We've found it. The missing ledger."

He gave a slow, sad nod. "I imagine you have. It was always the most dangerous piece of paper in the world."

"You killed him," Nell said, the words coming out flat and clear, cutting through the night air. "You pushed Owen Tarrant into the race."

For a moment, the mask flickered, a flicker of something like pity in his eyes. "He found out, you see," Vane said, his voice dropping to a confidential murmur. "About the Drainage Board money. He was a fool, trusting me with it. He went to the bank, asked for an audit. I saw the letter he'd written. It was all over for me."

He looked past Nell, his gaze fixed on the sluice gate wheel on the far bank. "That night, I waited. I had the key. I knew his habits. He always went down to check the race after midnight, when the water was quiet. I opened the gate, just a little, enough for a current to form. He was right there, looking over the edge. I just... gave him a push. He didn't even scream."

Nell felt a wave of nausea wash over her. The quiet, helpful man who had offered her tea and keys, who had hovered over her as she worked, was a monster. "And the lantern? The one we found in the reeds?"

"I hid it there after," Vane said with a shrug. "And the last ledger... I burned it. It had the incriminating figures. I was about to burn that one too, before you interrupted me. And your notes, my dear. I was most sorry to take them, but a girl can't be too careful with loose papers."

He took a step towards them. "Now, if you'll just give me that book, we can all forget this ever happened. I'll say you found it in the safe, I'll say I came to check on you. A tragic misunderstanding."

Kit stepped forward, placing himself between Vane and Nell. "It's over, Vane. You're trapped."

Vane's face darkened. "Trapped? By a clerk and a lock-keeper?" He laughed, a harsh, grating sound. "You have no idea what you're dealing with."

* * *

The sound of his laugh was the signal. Vane's eyes darted past them, to the far side of the sluice, then back to Nell. In that instant, he moved. He didn't lunge, but flowed sideways, a quick, decisive motion, and was already across the narrow walkway and halfway to the far bank. He didn't look back.

"He's going for the other gate!" Kit yelled.

Nell's mind, racing moments before with fear and triumph, cleared into sharp, cold focus. She saw it all in an instant: Vane on the far bank, opening the sluice there, letting the water rush in and flood the walkway they were standing on, trapping them in the middle of the weir. He would have the advantage.

Her eyes fell on the great iron wheel beside her, the one that controlled the gate. Kit's sluice key was in her pocket, a heavy, solid piece of brass. It was the only key that would work this wheel.

"Kit, the key!" she shouted, already pulling it from her pocket and thrusting it at him.

He took it without a word, his face grim. "Nell, no! It's too dangerous! He could push you in!"

"Just close it!" she screamed, her voice raw. "Stop the water!"

She didn't wait for him. She ran, not towards the walkway, but along the top of the bank, her boots slipping on the wet grass, until she reached the gate wheel. She braced her feet against the iron housing and grabbed the spokes, her fingers finding the grooves Kit had shown her. She put her shoulder into it, grunting with the effort, turning the heavy wheel. It resisted, grinding against its housing, but it moved. A low groan echoed from the gate below as it began to close.

Behind her, she heard the first rush of water as Kit closed the other gate. The roar of the race softened, the angry current subsiding. The walkway was no longer a death trap. She had stopped him. She had trapped him.

* * *

A moment later, Constable Dorrit arrived, panting and red-faced, Kit having fetched him from the police house. He held a lantern that cast long, dancing shadows, illuminating the scene in stark, dramatic relief. He took in Nell, panting and white-faced, leaning against the gate wheel, and Kit standing beside her, his expression grim. And there, on the far bank, Ambrose Vane, trapped like a cornered animal.

"Vane!" Dorrit bellowed, his voice shaking with a mixture of outrage and self-importance. "You are under arrest for the murder of Owen Tarrant!"

Vane didn't struggle. He stood there, his face pale in the moonlight, the genial mask finally and completely gone. He looked like a man who had known this moment was coming for a long time. He raised his hands in a gesture of weary surrender.

"Very well, Constable," he said, his voice devoid of its usual warmth. "You've won. I confess. I pushed him in."

Dorrit, flustered but triumphant, fumbled for his manacles and, with Kit's help, led the broken bookkeeper away. Nell sank to the damp grass, her legs suddenly unable to hold her. The ledger, which she had dropped in the struggle, lay at her feet, its pages splayed open in the dirt. The proof was done. The case was closed.

Kit was beside her in an instant, his arm wrapping around her shoulders, pulling her close. She leaned into him, the adrenaline draining away, leaving her trembling and weak. He was solid and real, a warm anchor in the cold, damp night.

"It's over," he whispered, his voice rough. "You did it. You trapped him."

Nell looked up at him, her eyes searching his face in the dim light. "I just turned a wheel," she said, her voice barely audible.

He smiled, a small, tired smile. "You did more than that. You were brilliant."

He leaned down and kissed her, not the awkward, hesitant kiss of the bridge, but a deep, sure kiss that held all the fear and relief of the night. When he pulled away, he rested his forehead against hers.

"Come on," he said softly. "Let's get you home."

He helped her to her feet, his arm a steady presence around her. They walked away from the mill, away from the sluice, away from the murder, towards the warm, quiet light of Aunt Maud's house, and the long, quiet night that stretched out before them, finally, without fear.
