# Chapter 13: Still_Water

The Plough Inn’s upstairs room smelled of damp wool and old woodsmoke, a scent Nell now associated with the business of death. Two weeks had passed since Ambrose Vane had been taken, and the air felt different, thinner, as if the town had been holding its breath. It was letting it out now. The coroner sat behind a table that was not quite as grand as the one used for the first inquest, and the few faces in the room were the same ones Nell had seen before, but their expressions had shifted from weary resignation to a kind of grim satisfaction. Constable Dorrit sat at the back, his uniform less rumpled, his bearing less shuffling. Kit was there, too, near the door, his quiet presence a steady point in the room.

The verdict was delivered without fuss: unlawful killing. Ambrose Vane. The name hung in the air, stripped of the geniality he had once worn like a second skin. It was done. The law had caught up with the truth, however slowly.

As the room began to empty, a small figure in black approached Nell. It was Ivy Tarrant. The brittle grief that had clung to her like a shroud since the first inquest had softened, though it had not vanished. Her eyes were red-rimmed but clear.

“Miss Ashby,” she said, her voice a fragile thing. “I wanted to thank you. Properly.” She clasped her hands together, her knuckles white. “They said it was an accident. An accident. I knew Owen would never have walked so close to that gate in the dark. He was careful. You saw what they couldn’t.” She paused, drawing a shaky breath. “They said you found the proof. I don’t know how, but I know you did. And for that… thank you.” A single tear traced a path down her cheek, and she turned away without another word, disappearing down the stairs.

Nell watched her go, a strange mix of pity and relief settling in her chest. The town could begin to heal now, with the truth laid bare.

* * *

The air had the clean, sharp bite of late autumn, and the sky above the fen was a vast, clear bowl of pale blue. The river, moving slowly through the lock, was a ribbon of dark, still water reflecting the pale light. Nell and Kit walked along the towpath, the crunch of gravel underfoot the only sound for a long while. The mill was behind them, a silent, brooding presence, but the fear that had once clung to the place was gone. In its place was a quiet that felt like peace.

“Heard from Dorrit,” Kit said, his hands thrust deep into the pockets of his thick woollen coat. “He said Vane won’t be getting out of Pentonville for a very long time. Said he’d been skimming the Drainage Board funds for years. Tarrant must have found the discrepancies in the old ledgers, the ones Vane thought he’d burned. The audit was the start of it all.” He shook his head slowly. “Seems a paltry reason for a man’s life.”

“Money does paltry things to people,” Nell said, kicking at a loose stone. It skipped across the path and vanished into the reeds. “It makes them small.”

They walked on in silence, the only sounds the water lapping gently at the bank and the distant cry of a curlew. The awkwardness that had sometimes hung between them had vanished, replaced by a comfortable ease. They had faced something terrible together, and in the aftermath, there was a simple, profound gratitude for the other’s presence.

They stopped by the lock. The great wooden gates were still, the sluice closed tight. Kit turned to face her, his eyes the same steady grey as the river.

“Nell,” he began, his voice quiet but clear. “The last few weeks… I’ve been thinking. About a great many things. About how things might have been.” He hesitated, searching for the right words. “I know we’ve… well, we’ve been through a great deal. But I’d like to think that what’s between us is more than just that. More than just shared danger and late nights.”

He looked at her, an earnest, hopeful look on his face. “I was wondering… if you would allow me to court you? Properly. Not in the middle of a murder inquiry. Just… Nell Ashby. If you’d be willing.”

Nell felt a warmth spread through her chest, a warmth that had nothing to do with her coat. She looked at him, at the honest concern in his eyes, the quiet strength she had come to rely on. It was a simple question, but it felt like the most important one she had ever been asked.

She smiled, a small, genuine smile. “Yes, Kit,” she said. “I would like that very much.”

The smile he gave her in return was slow and warm, and it reached all the way to his eyes. He didn’t take her hand, not yet, but he stood a little closer, the space between them humming with a quiet, hopeful promise.

* * *

The fire in Mr. Pell’s office was crackling cheerfully, and the room smelled of old paper, beeswax polish, and the damp fen air that came in whenever the door was opened. Nell sat opposite her employer, a cup of tea growing cold in her hands. Mr. Pell had been looking at her over the rims of his spectacles for some minutes now, a thoughtful, almost pleased expression on his face.

“Nell, my dear,” he began, folding his hands on the desk. “The business with Mr. Tarrant… it’s been a difficult time for the town, for all of us. But it has also been a time of great clarity. You have shown a remarkable aptitude for this work. A sharp eye for detail, a tenaciousness that I confess I did not expect, and a moral fortitude that is quite beyond your years.”

Nell felt a flush of heat rise to her cheeks. “I was only trying to do my job, Mr. Pell.”

“Precisely,” he said, leaning forward slightly. “And you did it exceptionally well. Better than exceptionally. You did what the law, for all its ponderousness, could not. You saw the truth.”

He picked up a pen from his desk and tapped it thoughtfully against a ledger. “My time, I am afraid, is drawing to a close. The rheumatism in my hands makes it increasingly difficult to manage the paperwork, and my eyes… well, they are not what they were. I have been giving thought to the future. To the future of this practice.”

He looked at her directly, his gaze keen. “I would like to offer you a position, Nell. As my articled clerk.”

Nell’s breath caught. It was more than she had dared to hope for, more than she had ever thought possible. To be a solicitor… it was a dream she had harboured since she was a girl, a dream she had always believed was meant for someone else, someone with a different background, different prospects.

“Me, sir?” she asked, her voice barely a whisper.

“Yes, you,” he said with a firm nod. “There would be articles to be signed, of course, and a period of study. It would not be easy. There is a great deal to learn. But I believe you have the capacity for it. I would be willing to take you on.”

He paused, then added, “It is not a common path for a woman. But then, nothing about you has ever been common, Miss Ashby. And the world, I find, is in need of uncommon solicitors.”

Nell looked from Mr. Pell’s kind, determined face to the window, where the river was visible, dark and still in the fading light. It was a quiet water, but she knew that beneath its surface, there was a current, a steady, powerful force. A current that was carrying her forward, towards a future she had only just begun to imagine.

She turned back to him, her own resolve settling into place as solid and certain as the lock gates. “Yes, Mr. Pell,” she said, her voice clear and strong. “I accept.”
