# Chapter 1: The Crossing

On the first of October, 1851, the river Lune ran high and brown past the village of Wenlow. Elspeth Marrow waited at the ferry landing with her father's silver compass in her coat pocket and twelve shillings in her purse, which was all the money she had in the world.

The ferry came out of the mist with Absalom Crane at the oar. Crane was seventy-one years old, and he rowed the way other old men walked, slowly and without ever stopping. The ferry was licensed to carry no more than three passengers at a time, and in forty years Crane had never once broken the licence.

He had three passengers that morning. Two were drovers in wet coats. The third was a tall woman in a blue cloak with a brass badge pinned at her throat.

"Captain Ida Thorne," Crane said, when the ferry bumped against the landing. "River warden. She wants a word with you, Elspeth."

The woman stepped off the ferry and looked Elspeth over. She had a weathered face and grey eyes that did not blink much. "You are Tobias Marrow's daughter."

"I am."

"Ten years ago your father was the pilot of the barge Margery, out of Stavely. The Margery went down in the Lune on a clear night with a full hold. Nobody ever found out why." Captain Thorne took a folded paper from her cloak. "Someone has written to the warden's office to say they know where her cargo went. The letter is signed with your father's name."

Elspeth stared at the paper. "My father is dead. He drowned with the Margery."

"So I have always been told," said Thorne. "I would like to know who wrote this letter."

Crane had not moved from his seat. He kept the ferry ledger under that seat, a fat book bound in oilcloth in which he had written every crossing he had ever made. Elspeth saw his hand go to it now, as if to make sure it was still there.

"Mr. Crane," Thorne said, "you ferried the Margery's crew across the night she sailed. It will be in your ledger."

"It may be," Crane said. "My eyes are bad for reading."

"Then I will read it for you."

"Not today, Captain." He pushed off from the landing without another word, and the ferry slid back into the mist with nobody aboard but Crane himself.

Thorne watched him go. "He is lying," she said, "or he is afraid. In my experience it is often both."

Elspeth walked up from the landing to the mill, where her brother Jonas worked the sluice gates. Jonas was sixteen, broad in the shoulders and quick to laugh, and he was covered in flour to the elbows when she found him.

She told him about the letter. He stopped laughing.

"Father's name," he said. "Written by who?"

"That is what the warden wants to know." Elspeth turned the silver compass over in her fingers. It had been their father's, and its needle had always pointed a little east of true. "She says the letter was posted in Stavely."

"Then we go to Stavely," Jonas said. "It is a full day's walk. We can leave at dawn."

"And the mill?"

"The mill has turned for three hundred years without me. It can turn for three more days."

That night Elspeth sat by the window of their cottage and counted her twelve shillings twice. Down on the river a lantern moved across the black water. It was Crane's ferry, crossing late, with one passenger she could not make out.
