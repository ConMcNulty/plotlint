# Chapter 2: The Heron

They set out at dawn on the second of October and walked east along the river road. The road to Stavely was a full day's walk, through wet fields and two villages that did not have names worth remembering. Past Stavely the same road went on to Saltmere on the coast, two days further, and nobody from Wenlow went there if they could help it. At the crossroads by the gibbet Elspeth took out the silver compass and checked their way, though she knew it already.

Jonas talked most of the morning and said nothing at all in the afternoon. By the time the roofs of Stavely showed over the hedges, the light was going and both of them were limping.

They took a room at the Heron, an inn by the bridge. The innkeeper was a heavy, careful man named Rook, who wiped every coin before he took it. Elspeth paid him three shillings for the room and their supper, which left her nine.

"Marrow," Rook said, when she gave him the name. "There was a Marrow on the river once. A pilot."

"Our father," said Jonas.

Rook nodded slowly. "The Margery. I remember the night. The whole town was at the bridge with lanterns, and nobody came up out of the water." He set two bowls of stew in front of them. "Eat. You look like you walked from Wenlow."

"We did."

"Then you'll want to know that the warden came in on horseback this afternoon. She is staying at the warden's house on Fuller Street."

Captain Thorne was indeed already in Stavely. She came to the Heron after supper, shaking rain from her cloak, and sat down at their table without asking.

"You walked," she said. "I rode. I have been to the post office. The letter was handed in by a boy who was paid a penny to carry it. He does not know who paid him, only that it was an old man with a bad cough who smelled of river water."

Elspeth thought of Crane at once, and saw from Thorne's face that she had thought of him too.

"There is something else," Thorne said. "The Margery was not carrying grain, whatever the shipping list says. She was carrying a strongbox with the Wenlow parish money in it, sixty pounds in gold, sent to the bank at Stavely for safekeeping. The strongbox was never found."

"And you think my father stole it," Elspeth said.

"I think somebody did. Your father is the easiest man to blame, because he is not here to argue."

Jonas put down his spoon. "He would not have."

"Then help me prove it," said Thorne. "I have just had word from Wenlow. Absalom Crane is ill. The doctor says it is his chest, and that he may not see the week out. If he knows anything, he will not know it for long."

Crane was at that moment in his cottage in Wenlow, a day's walk behind them, coughing into a handkerchief while the parish nurse boiled water on his stove. None of them knew yet how little time he had.

Elspeth lay awake a long time in the room above the bar. Below her the river went past the Heron the same way it went past Wenlow, brown and patient and full of things it would not give back.
