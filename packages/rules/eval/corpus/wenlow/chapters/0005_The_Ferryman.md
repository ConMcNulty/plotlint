# Chapter 5: The Ferryman

On the seventh of October they buried Absalom Crane in Wenlow churchyard, on the slope above the river where he could hear the water. Half the village came. The other half stayed home, and Elspeth noted which half each of the parish council belonged to.

After the service she walked down to the ferry landing with Captain Thorne. The strongbox was already locked in the vestry, with the vicar and two churchwardens sworn to it.

"The ledger," Thorne said. "It is time."

Elspeth had read it the night before, every page, by the lamp in their kitchen. She had read her father's letter too. They told the same story in two different hands.

She gave the ledger to Captain Thorne. Thorne held it for a moment without opening it, the way someone might hold a sleeping child.

"There are three names in it," Elspeth said. "One of them is Alderman Pask."

"I know," Thorne said. "I have suspected him for ten years. I could never prove it." She tucked the ledger inside her cloak. "Now I can."

At the landing the ferry was tied up where Crane had left it. Nobody had rowed it since he fell ill, and people who needed to cross had been walking the six miles round to the bridge at Stavely.

Jonas was sitting in the stern. He had Crane's oar across his knees and their father's silver compass on a string around his neck.

"Someone has to," he said, when Elspeth looked at him. "The parish will license me if the warden speaks for me. I asked the vicar."

"I will speak for you," said Thorne.

Three people were already waiting on the landing to cross: a farmer's wife with a basket of eggs, a boy with a dog, and an old man who had not been over the river in twenty years and wanted to see his sister before the winter. Jonas looked at them, and then at the brass plate on the ferry's bow, where the old licence was cut into the metal: NO MORE THAN THREE PASSENGERS.

"Three," he said. "Just right."

He took them across. Elspeth watched from the landing. He rowed badly at first and then less badly, and by the far bank he had found Crane's rhythm, slow and without ever stopping.

When he came back he was grinning. "Two pennies each," he said. "Six pennies. I am a man of business."

Elspeth laughed for the first time in a week. She had seven shillings in her purse, the strongbox was in the vestry, her father's name was clean, and the river went past Wenlow as it always had, brown and patient, giving back at last one of the things it had kept.
